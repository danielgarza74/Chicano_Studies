Part 1 · Mexicanos Today

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 1: How did demographic growth, geographic dispersal, and immigration status shape the national importance and challenges of Mexicanos after 2008?

IN MY WORDS: How many Mexicanos live in the United States, where do they live, and what challenges come with immigration status?

READING A | MEXICANOS TODAY

In 2012 about 33.7 million people of Mexican origin lived in the United States. They made up 64 percent of all Hispanics and 11 percent of the whole U.S. population. About 35 percent of them, or 11.4 million, were immigrants, by far the largest immigrant group in the country. Of these immigrants, about half (51 percent) were undocumented, a third were legal permanent residents, and the rest were naturalized citizens.

Illegal immigration from Mexico peaked in 2007 and then fell: unauthorized Mexican migrants dropped from 6.9 million that year to 5.6 million in 2015. The two main causes were the Great Recession of 2007–2009, which wiped out jobs, and tighter security on the border.

Mexicanos kept spreading out. By 2013 Mexicans were the largest immigrant group in 33 states. California and Texas still led, but the foreign-born population of the South grew 60 percent from 2000 to 2014.

Word help: undocumented = without legal papers · permanent resident = immigrant allowed to live here for good · naturalized = became a citizen

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 11, “A Demographic Overview,” pp. 376–378.

READING B | FLEEING CENTRAL AMERICA

As Mexican immigration slowed, more people came from Central America; between 2010 and 2014 Central American immigration rose 5 percent. Many were escaping violence. In 2013 the United Nations named Honduras the most violent country in the world, and in El Salvador the Mara Salvatrucha and 18th Street gangs had about 60,000 members combined.

Many travelers were children. In 2016 border officials caught nearly 60,000 unaccompanied children, most from Central America. Smugglers called coyotes charged about $7,500 a person, and the trip across Mexico was dangerous; nearly a third of women in one study reported sexual abuse on the way. Those who reached the border often asked for asylum, but about 80 percent of Central American requests were denied in 2016. Many spent months in detention, and most were sent back home.

Word help: unaccompanied = traveling without a parent · coyote = paid smuggler · asylum = protection for people fleeing danger

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 11, “A Demographic Overview,” pp. 377–378.

Part 1 continued · Living in the Homeland Security State

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 2: How did the post-9/11 homeland security state and anti-immigrant politics affect Mexicanos' civil liberties, mobility, and daily lives?

IN MY WORDS: How did fear, hard times, and new police powers change daily life for Mexicanos?

READING C | FEAR AFTER 9/11

The terrorist attacks of September 11, 2001, made many Americans more fearful of outsiders. Muslims felt the worst of the backlash, but Mexicans were targeted too, as fear of terrorists blended with fear of undocumented immigrants. Conservative talk radio hosts such as Rush Limbaugh stirred anger, and citizen militias began patrolling the border. The best known, the Minutemen Civil Defense Corps, was founded in 2004.

The Great Recession of 2007–2009 made things worse. Many Mexicanos had been given risky home loans and lost their homes when the housing bubble burst; Latinos lost more household wealth than any other group. Yet as unemployment doubled, many Americans blamed Mexicans for taking jobs, even though immigration from Mexico was slowing as work disappeared.

Word help: hysteria = extreme, uncontrolled fear · militia = armed citizen group · recession = period when the economy shrinks

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 11, “Anti-Mexican Hysteria after 9/11,” pp. 378–379.

Part 1 continued · New Police Powers and the Border

READING D | THE HOMELAND SECURITY STATE

After 9/11, beginning with the USA PATRIOT Act, the federal government greatly expanded its police powers. Scholars call the result the homeland security state, and sealing the border with Mexico was one of its main goals. In 2006 President George W. Bush signed the Secure Fence Act, authorizing a 700-mile border fence.

Local police were enlisted too. Under “287(g) agreements,” state and local officers could hand unauthorized immigrants over to ICE for detention or deportation; Sheriff Joe Arpaio of Maricopa County, Arizona, became famous for using this power. A 2008 program, Secure Communities, let local police share data on immigrants with Homeland Security and the FBI. Critics charged that these programs led to racial profiling and civil rights violations.

Daily life grew harder. Crossing became deadlier: about 1,200 people went missing from mid-2016 to mid-2017, many lost to dehydration or cold, and coyotes and drug cartels preyed on travelers. Those who made it lived in fear of ICE raids, especially day laborers.

Word help: ICE = federal immigration police · racial profiling = targeting people because of their race · detention = being held in custody

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 11, “Anti-Mexican Hysteria after 9/11,” pp. 379–381.

Part 2 · The Obama Years: Opportunity and Enforcement

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 3: How did the Obama administration combine expanded opportunities such as DACA with record enforcement and the failure of comprehensive immigration reform?

IN MY WORDS: How did the Obama years bring both new chances and record deportations?

READING E | REFORM BLOCKED, ENFORCEMENT EXPANDED

Barack Obama won 67 percent of the Latino vote in 2008, partly by promising comprehensive immigration reform with a path to citizenship. Congress never passed it. Republicans refused to work with him, and after they won the House in 2010 they blocked his major plans.

Obama kept and expanded tough enforcement. Border Patrol agents grew from 12,349 in 2006 to 19,828 in 2016, backed by sensors and drones. Deportations peaked at 409,000 in 2012, and his administration deported more than 2.7 million people in eight years, mostly Latinos. Many detainees waited months without a hearing, often in private detention centers. In November 2014 he shifted priorities to “felons, not families,” and deportations fell.

States passed their own laws. In 2010 Arizona's SB 1070 let police arrest unauthorized immigrants and, critics said, encouraged racial profiling. Beginning May 1, 2010, huge rallies led by immigrants and young “Dreamers” spread across the country.

Word help: comprehensive = covering everything · path to citizenship = a way to become a citizen · deport = force to leave the country

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 11, “The Obama Presidency,” pp. 381–384.

READING F | DACA AND NEW GAINS

Unable to move Congress, Obama used his own power. On June 15, 2012, he created Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals (DACA). Undocumented young people who were in school, had graduated, or had served in the military received two years of protection from deportation, plus work permits and the right to get driver's licenses. More than 752,000 “Dreamers” had received DACA by 2016.

Mexicanos gained in other ways too. Obama named Ken Salazar secretary of the interior and Hilda Solís secretary of labor, and Sonia Sotomayor became the first Latina on the Supreme Court. The Latino high school dropout rate fell from 28 percent in 2000 to 14 percent in 2012, and more than ten million uninsured Latinos gained coverage through the Affordable Care Act (Obamacare). Latino support for Obama rose to 73 percent in 2012.

Word help: deferred = put off until later · Dreamers = young immigrants brought here as children · executive action = a president acting without Congress

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 11, “The Obama Presidency,” pp. 384–388.

Part 3 · The Era of Trump

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 4: How did the Trump era transform immigration enforcement, political rhetoric, and Mexican American resistance, and what did it reveal about the future of integration and equality?

IN MY WORDS: What changed under Trump, and how did Mexicanos and their allies push back?

READING G | WORDS AND ENFORCEMENT

During his 2016 campaign, Donald Trump called Mexican immigrants rapists and drug dealers and promised a wall along the whole border to keep out “bad hombres.” He said that Judge Gonzalo Curiel, who was born in Indiana, could not treat him fairly because he was “Mexican,” and he had Univisión anchor Jorge Ramos removed from a press conference. Exit polls gave him between 18 and 29 percent of the Hispanic vote.

In office he moved fast. A January 25, 2017, order told Homeland Security to start building the wall and hire 5,000 more Border Patrol agents. ICE tripled in size with 10,000 new agents, and workplace raids resumed. Every undocumented immigrant, not just criminals, became a target. From late January to late April 2017, immigration arrests rose 38 percent, to 41,318.

Word help: rhetoric = the way leaders speak to persuade · executive order = a president's official command · raid = surprise arrest operation

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 11, “The Era of Trump,” pp. 388–395.

READING H | ENDING PROTECTIONS, FIGHTING BACK

The administration also attacked protections. In June 2017 it canceled DAPA, an Obama plan to shield undocumented parents of citizens. On September 5, 2017, it announced that DACA would end, leaving the Dreamers' future up to Congress. The proposed RAISE Act would have cut green cards by about half and ended the preference for reuniting families.

Many fought back. About 600 “sanctuary” cities and counties refused to hold immigrants for federal agents, and courts often ruled for immigrants. In October 2017 Governor Jerry Brown signed SB 54, making California a statewide sanctuary. Senate leader Kevin de León wrote it, and Attorney General Xavier Becerra and Secretary of State Alex Padilla defended immigrants too.

Gonzales sees hard times ahead but some hope. In a 2017 poll, 61 percent of Americans favored letting unauthorized immigrants stay and apply for citizenship, and most opposed the wall. Sheriff Arpaio was convicted in 2017, and immigrants were reviving struggling towns in the Midwest and South.

Word help: sanctuary = place of protection · green card = permission to live here permanently · rescind = cancel

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 11, “The Era of Trump,” pp. 395–399.
