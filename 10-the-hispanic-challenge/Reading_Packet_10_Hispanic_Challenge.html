Part 1 · A National Presence

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 1: How did immigration, population growth, geographic dispersal, and assimilation make Mexicanos a central presence in American life after 1994?

IN MY WORDS: How did new immigrants and new places to live make Mexicanos part of the whole nation's story?

READING A | A NATION OF NEWCOMERS

Around 2000 the United States was in the middle of an immigration wave as large as the one a century earlier, but most newcomers now came from Latin America, and Mexico sent by far the most. The number of Mexican immigrants rose from about 4.2 million in 1990 to 8.1 million in 2000 and 11.7 million in 2007, six times more than from China, the next country. Perhaps 55 percent of them were undocumented. The wider Latino population reached about 47 million in 2007, 15.5 percent of the nation.

Why did so many leave? Mexico still could not offer many people a decent living. After NAFTA began in 1994, cheap U.S. corn poured into Mexico, and perhaps two million small farmers were pushed off their land. In December 1994 the peso lost 40 percent of its value, and in 2005 nearly half of Mexicans lived on less than four dollars a day. Drug cartels also brought violence to border cities such as Tijuana and Ciudad Juárez.

Word help: undocumented = without legal papers · NAFTA = 1994 free-trade pact of Canada, Mexico, and the U.S. · cartel = criminal drug organization

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 10, “Mexicanos Galore,” pp. 330–335.

READING B | NEW DESTINATIONS, DEEP ROOTS

Mexicanos were also living in new places. Most still lived in Texas or California, but by 2000 the fastest growth was in the Midwest, the South, the Mountain West, and the Northeast. In Iowa, meatpacking plants had moved to small towns such as Postville and Marshalltown, and the state's Hispanic population jumped 153 percent in the 1990s. Chicago's Pilsen and Little Village became a “Little Mexico,” and New York City had about 600,000 Mexicanos by the mid-2000s, most from the state of Puebla.

The biggest surprise was the South, where the Mexicano population tripled in the 1990s. Jobs drew people there: work in the growing suburbs of Atlanta, Charlotte, and Nashville, and in poultry plants in places such as Siler City, North Carolina, and northwest Arkansas, home of Tyson Foods.

Newcomers put down roots. With the border harder to cross, more stayed for good and brought their families. Many kept close ties to Mexico, sending home about $23 billion in 2007 and forming more than 600 hometown associations. Mexico allowed dual citizenship after 1998.

Word help: remittances = money sent home · hometown association = club that helps a migrant's home village · dual citizenship = citizen of two countries

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 10, “Mexicanos Galore,” pp. 335–340.

Part 1 continued · Stemming the Tide

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 2: How did border militarization and enforcement policies attempt to stem immigration, and what consequences did they produce?

IN MY WORDS: How did the government try to close the border, and what happened as a result?

READING C | A TOUGHER BORDER

As immigration rose, many Americans believed the country had lost control of its southern border, and the government acted. In 1993 the Clinton administration launched Operation Gatekeeper, increasing the number of Border Patrol agents by about 45 percent. A 1996 law known as IIRIRA added more agents and surveillance technology, raised penalties for smugglers and migrants, and made immigrants who committed felonies and some lesser crimes subject to deportation. After the attacks of September 11, 2001, immigration came under the new Department of Homeland Security.

The Fence Act of 2006 began nearly 700 more miles of fencing. By then about 12,000 agents guarded the border, compared with 3,700 twenty years earlier.

The results were not what leaders expected. Crossers shifted to remote, dangerous stretches of desert, and migrant deaths along the border rose from 261 in 1998 to 409 in 2003. Because returning home had become risky, many migrants who once came and went with the seasons simply stayed.

Word help: militarization = making an area more like a military zone · deportation = forcing someone to leave the country · surveillance = close watching

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 10, “Stemming the Tide,” pp. 340–343.

Part 2 · Backlash and Response

CORE OBJECTIVES

FQ 3: What fears, rhetoric, and political campaigns fueled the new nativism, and how did it affect Mexican immigrants and U.S.-born Mexicanos?

FQ 4: How did attacks on immigrant rights generate grassroots mobilization, protest, voter registration, and coalition building?

IN MY WORDS: What fed the anti-immigrant backlash, and how did Mexicanos fight back?

READING D | THE NEW NATIVISM

Fear of immigrants grew into a broad anti-immigrant movement. In 1994 California voters passed Proposition 187 with 59 percent of the vote. It denied undocumented immigrants and their families health care, welfare, and public schooling. Proposition 209 (1996) ended affirmative action, and Proposition 227 (1998) ended bilingual education. FBI reports show that anti-Hispanic hate crimes rose 25 percent from 2004 to 2008.

Other voices added fuel. Vigilante groups such as the Minutemen patrolled the border on their own. FAIR, the largest anti-immigration group, had about 200,000 members, and cable host Lou Dobbs attacked immigrants night after night on CNN.

The Republican Party was split. Its voting base wanted to cut legal immigration and end illegal immigration completely. Business leaders, however, wanted a steady supply of cheap workers. In 2007 Republican Senate leaders let a bipartisan reform bill die. Some towns passed their own laws; Hazleton, Pennsylvania, tried to punish landlords who rented to undocumented immigrants.

Word help: nativism = hostility toward immigrants · vigilante = private citizen who enforces the law on his own · bipartisan = backed by both parties

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 10, “Barbarians at the Gate: The New Nativism,” pp. 341, 343–346.

READING E | THE GIANT AWAKENS

Mexicanos saw these attacks as aimed at their whole community, not only at immigrants. About 80 percent of Hispanic voters opposed Propositions 187 and 209. In 1994, 100,000 people marched in Los Angeles against Proposition 187. Courts later struck down its main parts because immigration is a federal power, and MALDEF led that legal fight.

The largest protests came in spring 2006, against a House bill sponsored by Rep. F. James Sensenbrenner. It would have made illegal entry a felony and punished anyone who helped undocumented immigrants. About 100,000 people marched in Chicago on March 10 and half a million in Los Angeles on March 25. On May 1, about one million joined “A Day without an Immigrant,” a boycott and day of marches.

Spanish-language radio hosts, such as Los Angeles DJ “El Cucuy,” urged listeners to march. MALDEF, the National Council of La Raza (NCLR), and Milwaukee's Voces de la Frontera helped organize. For the first time, many undocumented immigrants protested in public, and U.S.-born and immigrant Mexicanos marched side by side.

Word help: mobilization = organizing people to act · felony = serious crime · boycott = refusing to work or buy as a protest

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 10, “The Awakening Giant: Grassroots Mobilization,” pp. 348–350.

Part 3 · Votes and Inequality

CORE OBJECTIVES

FQ 5: How did electoral gains, party loyalties, and changing coalitions expand and limit Mexican American political power?

FQ 6: Why did major socioeconomic inequalities persist despite demographic growth and political gains?

IN MY WORDS: How did Mexicano voting power grow, and why did many still stay poor?

READING F | VOTES AND COALITIONS

Both parties began to court Latino voters. President Bill Clinton won 72 percent of the Latino vote in 1996, after Republicans backed Proposition 187 and English as the official language. George W. Bush campaigned in Spanish, stressed family and religious values, and promised immigration reform. He won about 37 percent of Latinos in 2000 and between 37 and 44 percent in 2004. In 2006, however, a new Republican anti-immigrant campaign, policies that favored the rich, and the Iraq War drove Latinos back: Democrats won 69 percent.

A high point came in 2005, when Antonio Villaraigosa became the first Hispanic mayor of Los Angeles since 1872, with help from a broad coalition.

Limits remained. In 2004 Latinos were about 14 percent of the population but cast only 6 percent of the votes. Many were not citizens, about a third were under 18, and some had lost voting rights because of prison records. Coalitions were hard to build: conservative Cuban Americans voted Republican, and competition for jobs strained relations with African Americans.

Word help: coalition = groups working together · turnout = share of people who vote · eligible = allowed

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 10, “The Awakening Giant: Electoral Politics,” pp. 350–361.

READING G | WHY INEQUALITY LASTED

Growth and new political leaders did not end poverty. In 2000 about two-thirds of Hispanics were working class. Gonzales points to three barriers. Globalization replaced factory jobs with low-wage service work. A steady flow of poor newcomers kept pulling group averages down. And public policy favored the wealthy; Republican leaders, for example, fought increases in the minimum wage.

Education lagged. In 2006 only 59 percent of Hispanics age 25 and older had finished high school. In Huron, California, a farm town that was 98 percent Latino, not one resident had a college degree in 2000.

Health suffered too. About 37 percent of Mexican Americans were obese in 2007, linked to fast food, little exercise, and depression, and about one-third had no health insurance. Still, the middle class grew: Latino buying power neared $600 billion by 2005, and new Latino businesses were starting at three times the national rate.

Word help: globalization = linking of the world's economies · minimum wage = lowest legal pay · obese = seriously overweight

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 10, “Socioeconomic Realities,” pp. 361–364.

Part 4 · Sports and Belonging

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 7: How have sports and popular culture served as avenues for assimilation, community solidarity, ethnic pride, and public recognition?

IN MY WORDS: How did sports build pride and acceptance for Mexicanos?

READING H | SPORTS AND BELONGING

Next to school, sports have long been a main path into American life. Mexicanos played and followed sports to win acceptance, to bond with one another, and to build self-esteem and ethnic pride. Boxing came first. Fighters such as Bert Colima, “el ídolo de Whittier,” became heroes across the Southwest, and later champions included Oscar de la Hoya. In 1984 Paul Gonzales became the first Mexican American to win an Olympic boxing title.

In 1981 Los Angeles Dodgers pitcher Fernando Valenzuela, from Sonora, Mexico, became the first rookie to win the Cy Young Award. “Fernandomania” united Mexicans on both sides of the border, who claimed him as their own.

Mexicanos also starred in football; Anthony Muñoz is in the NFL Hall of Fame. Pancho Gonzales was the top American professional tennis player of the 1950s and 1960s, and golfers Lee Treviño and Nancy López both entered golf's Hall of Fame.

Word help: assimilation = becoming part of the mainstream · solidarity = unity · recognition = public respect

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 10, “Popular Culture: Mexicanos and the Wide World of Sports,” pp. 365–367.

READING I | ANSWERING “THE HISPANIC CHALLENGE”

In 2004 Harvard political scientist Samuel Huntington published an influential article, “The Hispanic Challenge.” He claimed Mexican immigrants were unlike earlier immigrants: they lived next door to their homeland, arrived in huge numbers, and held Catholic and Indian values that, he said, made it almost impossible for them to assimilate.

Gonzales rejects this view and points to the children and grandchildren of immigrants. Latinos usually shift from Spanish to English within one generation. More Mexican Americans live in suburbs and have better schooling and jobs. About half of third-generation Hispanics who married in 2000 married non-Latinos. Latinos are overrepresented in military combat positions, and Latino men have the highest work-force participation of any group, about 80 percent. A 2007 Pew survey found most Hispanics content with their lives and hopeful about their children's future.

Word help: assimilate = blend into the mainstream · third generation = grandchildren of immigrants · overrepresented = more than their share

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 10, “Popular Culture: Mexicanos and the Wide World of Sports,” pp. 367–370.
