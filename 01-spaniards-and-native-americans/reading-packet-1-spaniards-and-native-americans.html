Two Worlds Before 1519

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 1: How did Iberia's multicultural past, the Reconquista, political unification, and Atlantic competition shape Spain's motives and capacity for overseas exploration and conquest?

IN MY WORDS: What in Spain's past made Spain want to explore, and what made it able to?

Reading aIberia's Mixed Past and the Reconquista

Spain sits on the Iberian Peninsula, a land bridge between Europe and Africa, so many peoples settled there over the centuries. The Romans ruled for hundreds of years. They left behind their laws, the Latin language that later grew into Spanish, and Christianity. In 711, Muslim Moors from North Africa crossed into Iberia and stayed for more than 750 years. Moorish and Jewish scholars turned cities such as Córdoba into famous centers of learning in math, philosophy, and the arts.

Meanwhile, small Christian kingdoms in the north fought for centuries to drive the Moors out. Spaniards call this long struggle the Reconquista (Reconquest). Every campaign was treated as a holy crusade, so religion and loyalty to the nation became tied together. The result was a fierce, fighting Catholic faith. Later, that same zeal pushed Spaniards to convert the Native peoples of the Americas.

Word help: peninsula = land almost surrounded by water · Moors = Muslims from North Africa · crusade = a holy war fought for religion · zeal = strong, eager devotion

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 1, “The Spaniards,” pp. 10–14.

Reading BUnity, Rivalry, and Columbus

In 1469, Prince Ferdinand of Aragón married Princess Isabella of Castilla, and their two kingdoms were soon joined. In January 1492, these “Catholic Kings” captured Granada, the last Muslim stronghold, and ended the Reconquista. Two months later, Jews who refused to convert were forced to leave. Spain was now one strongly Catholic state, and it faced the Atlantic, ready to send out ships.

Spain also had a rival next door. Portugal got a head start when Prince Henry the Navigator backed voyages down the African coast in search of gold and a sea route to the spices of Asia. In 1498, Vasco da Gama reached India.

Christopher Columbus, a sailor from Genoa in Italy, believed he could reach Asia by sailing west. Queen Isabella paid for the trip. On October 12, 1492, his crew sighted land in the Bahamas. He returned with captured natives and just enough gold to start a rush for New World riches.

Word help: stronghold = a fortified place · rival = competitor · convert = change to a new religion

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 1, “The Spaniards,” pp. 14–17.

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 2: How did migration, agriculture, religion, warfare, and exchange shape Mesoamerican civilizations, and how did the Aztec Empire create both power and vulnerability before 1519?

IN MY WORDS: How did the peoples of Mexico build great civilizations, and what made the Aztecs both strong and weak?

Reading CFrom Farmers to Cities

Most scholars think the first Americans were hunters who crossed from Siberia to Alaska during the last Ice Age and then spread south over thousands of years. In the highlands of central Mexico, people began to grow maize (corn) around 5000 BCE. Steady harvests let them stop wandering, settle in villages, and grow in number. Mesoamerica became one of the most crowded regions of the Americas.

The Olmecs built the first ceremonial centers around 1200 BCE. Priests and warrior nobles ruled, merchants traded over long distances, and a religion with many gods and human sacrifice shaped daily life. Later peoples shared these beliefs. Teotihuacán, near today's Mexico City, grew to perhaps 200,000 people and spread its power through trade. The Maya studied the stars and created a true writing system, but their warrior-kings ruled separate city-states that fought constantly and never united.

Word help: maize = corn · ceremonial center = a place for religious rituals · sacrifice = an offering to the gods, sometimes a human life · city-state = a city that rules itself and nearby land

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 1, “The Amerindians,” pp. 18–22.

Reading DThe Aztec Empire

The Aztecs, who called themselves the Méxica, arrived in the Valley of Mexico in the 1200s as poor wanderers. A prophecy said they should settle where they saw an eagle on a cactus holding a serpent. In 1325 they began building Tenochtitlán on an island in Lake Texcoco. By 1519 the city held at least 250,000 people, and Spanish soldiers later compared its canals to Venice.

Through a Triple Alliance with two other cities, the Aztecs conquered an empire of more than six million people in southern Mexico. Roads and trade tied it together. Conquered peoples had to send tribute, including captives to be sacrificed to Huitzilopochtli, the war god.

This power had a weak spot. Most subject peoples had been conquered by force and were ruled through fear. Some, such as the Tlaxcalans, had kept their independence and hated the Aztecs. They were ready to side with any enemy strong enough to beat them.

Word help: prophecy = a prediction · alliance = a partnership · tribute = payments a ruler forces conquered people to make · subject peoples = people ruled by a stronger power

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 1, “The Amerindians” and “The Conquest of Mexico,” pp. 23–24, 27.

The Conquest of Mexico

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 3: Why were Cortés and his Indigenous allies able to defeat the Aztec Empire, and how did the conquest reshape Mexico's demographic, cultural, and racial foundations?

IN MY WORDS: Why did a few hundred Spaniards and their Indian allies win, and what did the conquest change?

Reading ECortés Marches on Tenochtitlán

In February 1519, Hernán Cortés sailed from Cuba with eleven ships and about 600 men. After landing near present-day Veracruz, he destroyed his ships, according to legend, so no one could turn back. A Nahuatl-speaking enslaved young woman, Malintzin (called Doña Marina or La Malinche), was given to the Spaniards. As their guide and interpreter, she helped them survive battle after battle.

Emperor Moctezuma II held back. A legend said the light-skinned god Quetzalcóatl would return from the east, and the Spaniards arrived at about the predicted time. In November 1519, Moctezuma let them march into his island capital with almost no fight, giving away its strong position in the lake. Cortés soon made him a prisoner and used him to collect gold.

The Méxica rebelled, and Moctezuma was fatally injured. On la noche triste (the sad night), July 1, 1520, the Spaniards fled the city and hundreds died. The survivors found safety with their Tlaxcalan allies.

Word help: interpreter = a person who translates speech · legend = an old story that may not be true · fatally = causing death

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 1, “The Conquest of Mexico,” pp. 24–26.

Reading FHow the Empire Fell

Months later, Cortés came back with Tlaxcalans and other Native allies, about 200,000 in all. Indian workers built thirteen boats for an attack across the lake. After a three-month siege, Tenochtitlán fell on August 13, 1521. About 20,000 defenders died, and the city was torn down stone by stone.

Fewer than a thousand Spaniards took part in the final attack, but they had advantages. Horses amazed the Aztecs, who at first thought horse and rider were one animal, and huge war dogs terrified them. Cannon, crossbows, guns, and steel armor outmatched Native weapons. Just as important, many subject peoples hated the Aztecs and fought alongside the Spaniards.

Disease struck as well. In 1520, smallpox swept through the empire and killed tens of thousands. Native Americans had no immunity to European illnesses, and one historian argues the Spaniards could not have won without the epidemic.

Word help: siege = surrounding a city to force it to surrender · immunity = the body's natural protection against a disease · epidemic = a disease that spreads to many people at once

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 1, “The Conquest of Mexico,” pp. 26–27.

Reading GA New People

The conquest brought terrible loss. European diseases kept spreading, and over time the Indian population fell by about 90 percent. Temples were torn down, and Spain imposed its rule and its religion.

Yet the conquest was also a beginning. The Spanish Crown and Church allowed marriage between Spaniards and Indians, and Cortés himself took Malintzin as his partner. This mixing, called mestizaje, created a mestizo people; most Mexicans today are mestizos. Spain also left behind Catholicism and the Spanish language. Mexico now has the largest Catholic and the largest Spanish-speaking population in the world.

Indian ways did not disappear. They lived on in family life, legends, festivals, and popular religion, especially among ordinary working people. Today many Mexicans and Mexican Americans proudly claim their Indian roots.

Word help: impose = force on others · mestizaje = the mixing of peoples · mestizo = a person of mixed Spanish and Indian ancestry

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 1, “The Conquest of Mexico,” pp. 27–30.
