A New Nation Loses Its Grip

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 1: How did the struggle for Mexican independence and the new republic's political and economic instability weaken control over the Far North?

IN MY WORDS: How did the fight for independence and the troubles that followed leave the Far North unprotected?

Reading AMexico Wins Independence

In New Spain, Spanish-born officials called peninsulares held the top jobs in government and the Church. American-born criollos (creoles) resented being shut out. When Napoleon's France conquered Spain in 1808, the whole empire was thrown into chaos.

On September 16, 1810, the priest Miguel Hidalgo called on the people of Dolores to rise up. His army of poor farmers grew fast, but looting and killing frightened wealthy creoles, who turned against the revolt. Trained Spanish troops crushed it, and Hidalgo was executed in 1811.

Independence finally came in 1821, led by conservatives. A creole officer, Agustín de Iturbide, broke with Spain and made himself emperor. He was overthrown in 1823, and Mexico became a republic. For decades afterward, liberals and conservatives fought for control, and General Antonio López de Santa Anna rose to dominate the country's politics.

Word help: peninsular = a Spanish-born person in the colony · creole = a person of Spanish ancestry born in the Americas · republic = a nation with elected leaders and no king

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 3, “Mexican Independence” and “California,” pp. 66–68, 70.

Reading BTrouble on the Frontier

People in the Far North mostly watched the independence war from far away, but they felt its effects. To fight the rebels, Spain pulled many soldiers out of the northern presidios (forts). The war also wrecked Mexico's silver mines, its main source of money, so the new nation could not afford to rebuild frontier defenses.

With the forts weak, Apache bands and other tribes went back to raiding. Peace agreements Spain had made with the Comanche, Ute, Navajo, and Jicarilla Apache fell apart in the 1820s. American traders made things worse by selling guns to Native raiders and buying the goods they took.

At the same time, more and more Americans were moving into Mexico's northern lands. In the end, they would become an even bigger threat than the raids.

Word help: presidio = a frontier fort · rebels = people fighting against their government · raid = a quick surprise attack to steal or destroy

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 3, “Mexican Independence,” pp. 68–69.

FQ 2: How did mission secularization, ranching, foreign trade, and social hierarchy transform Mexican California?

FQ 3: How did geographic isolation, trade, class relations, and Indigenous conflict shape New Mexico under Mexican rule?

FQ 4: Why did Arizona stagnate during the Mexican period, and how did Apache resistance and weak governmental support affect its settlements?

IN MY WORDS: How did life change in California, New Mexico, and Arizona after 1821, and why did some places grow while others struggled?

Reading CMexican California

About 3,200 Californios lived in California in 1821. They disliked rule from far-off Mexico City and often rebelled, usually without bloodshed, against governors sent from the capital.

Between 1834 and 1845, the missions were secularized: their huge lands were taken from the Church. The law said half should go to the mission Indians and half to the government. Instead, about 200 families ended up with nearly all of it, and governors handed out about 500 rancho grants. A wealthy rancho elite was born.

Ranchos raised horses and great herds of cattle. Ships from New England traded manufactured goods for cattle hides and tallow (fat used for soap and candles). At the top of society were the mostly white rancheros, less than 10 percent of the people; women could own land, and 55 grants went to women. Mestizo soldiers, craftsmen, and vaqueros came next. Indians were at the bottom, working for rancheros in conditions close to serfdom.

Word help: secularize = take out of Church control · rancho = a large cattle ranch · vaquero = cowboy · serfdom = forced labor tied to the land

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 3, “California,” pp. 69–73.

Reading DNew Mexico and Arizona

New Mexico was about 1,600 miles from Mexico City, so it ran its own affairs. But when Apache and Navajo raids grew worse, the government sent no help. In 1837 rebels killed Albino Pérez, an unpopular governor sent from the capital; Manuel Armijo crushed the revolt and then dominated New Mexico's politics.

Sheep ranching and farming along the Rio Grande were the base of the economy. For years, Chihuahua merchants controlled trade and charged high prices. Then in 1821 William Becknell, a Missouri trader, opened the Santa Fe Trail. Cheaper American goods poured in, and rich New Mexican families such as the Armijos later took over much of the trade. The gap between ricos and pobres widened, and about 9,000 Pueblo Indians, some working almost as slaves, were at the bottom.

Arizona did poorly. Its only real settlements were the presidios of Tubac and Tucson. Apache raids were so fierce that the mission of San Xavier del Bac was abandoned in 1829, Tubac was emptied by 1848, and the population kept falling.

Word help: isolated = cut off from others · monopoly = complete control of a trade · ricos and pobres = rich and poor

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 3, “New Mexico” and “Arizona,” pp. 73–77.

How Was Texas Lost?

CORE OBJECTIVES

FQ 5: How did Mexican immigration policy and the influx of Anglo settlers undermine Mexican authority in Texas?

FQ 6: How did competing cultural assumptions about land, law, race, religion, and government intensify conflict between Mexicanos and Anglo Americans?

IN MY WORDS: Why did inviting Americans into Texas backfire on Mexico?

Reading EInviting Americans Into Texas

In 1821 most Tejanos lived around San Antonio, Goliad, Nacogdoches, and Laredo, and Comanche raids kept the province weak. Few Mexicans wanted to move there, but Americans did. So Mexico tried a new plan: invite American settlers and manage them through agents called empresarios.

Stephen F. Austin was the best known. He brought about 300 families, each given about 5,000 acres along the Brazos River on condition that they obey Mexican law, learn Spanish, and become Catholic. Thousands more Americans followed, many without permission. By 1830, Anglos outnumbered Mexicans in Texas 25,000 to 4,000.

The two groups differed in language, religion, and ideas about government. Race mattered most: about three-quarters of the Anglos were Southerners committed to slavery, and many looked down on Mexicans. In 1830–1832, Mexico banned new American immigration, encouraged Mexican families to settle, and raised tariffs. It came too late, and the laws only angered Anglo-Texans.

Word help: Tejano = a Mexican resident of Texas · empresario = an agent paid to bring in settlers · tariff = a tax on imported goods

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 3, “Texas” and “The Clash of Cultures,” pp. 77–79.

FQ 7: What political, economic, and cultural forces caused the Texas Revolt, and how did Tejanos and Anglo settlers experience the conflict differently?

IN MY WORDS: Why did Texas rebel, and did Tejanos and Anglos get the same results?

Reading FThe Texas Revolt

In 1834, Santa Anna and the centralists took over Mexico's government and set out to crush local self-rule everywhere. Their harsh moves angered Anglo-Texans and even many Tejanos. Sam Houston, who wanted independence right away, and Stephen Austin, who had favored compromise, now joined forces, and the revolt began in 1835.

On March 6, 1836, Santa Anna's army of about 5,000 stormed the Alamo, an old mission in San Antonio, and killed 187 defenders, eight of them Mexicans. Tejanos such as Juan Seguín and José Antonio Navarro fought for Texas, though most Tejanos stayed neutral. A month later, Houston's army surprised Santa Anna at San Jacinto and captured him, ending the war.

Texas became an independent republic, but Tejanos were soon treated as foreigners in their own land. Anglos took their lands and attacked them, and many fled south. Even Juan Seguín was driven out.

Word help: centralist = someone who wants a strong national government · neutral = not taking sides · republic = an independent nation with elected leaders

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 3, “The Texas Revolt,” pp. 81–83.

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 8: How did Manifest Destiny, territorial ambition, and the annexation of Texas lead to the Mexican-American War, and what did the war cost Mexico?

IN MY WORDS: Why did the United States go to war with Mexico, and what did Mexico lose?

Reading GThe Road to War

By the 1840s many Americans believed in Manifest Destiny, the idea that the United States was meant to spread across the continent. Poet Walt Whitman called it “the great mission of peopling the New World with a noble race.” James K. Polk won the presidency in 1844 promising to gain Texas and Oregon. Texas was annexed in early 1845, and angry Mexican leaders talked of war.

Polk also wanted California. Late in 1845 he tried to buy it, but Mexico refused even to meet his agent, John Slidell. Polk then turned to a border dispute. Texas claimed the Rio Grande as its border; Mexico said it was the Nueces River, about 150 miles north. In March 1846 Polk ordered General Zachary Taylor's army into the land between the rivers. When Mexican troops attacked Taylor's soldiers in April, Polk asked Congress for war. Some Americans, including Congressman Abraham Lincoln, doubted that the land was really American.

Word help: Manifest Destiny = the belief that the U.S. was meant to expand · annex = add territory to a country · dispute = a disagreement

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 3, “The Mexican War,” pp. 84–85, 91.

Reading HWar and the Treaty

U.S. forces invaded northern Mexico, took Santa Fe in 1846, and fought their way into California, where Californio lancers won a battle at San Pascual before surrendering in January 1847. In September 1847, General Winfield Scott captured Mexico City. The war cost about 50,000 Mexican lives.

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, signed February 2, 1848, forced Mexico to give up California and New Mexico and to accept the loss of Texas. Mexico lost 947,570 square miles, almost half its land, though less than 1 percent of its people. The United States paid $15 million. About 100,000 Mexicans lived in the lost lands, and only about 2,000 moved south. Article IX promised those who stayed “all the rights of citizens of the United States.”

Word help: treaty = a formal agreement between nations · surrender = give up · cession = land handed over to another country

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 3, “The Mexican War,” pp. 85–88.
