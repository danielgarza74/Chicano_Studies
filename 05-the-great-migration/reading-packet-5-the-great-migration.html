Part 1 · Why Did a Million Mexicans Move North?

CORE OBJECTIVES

FQ 1: What push-and-pull forces drove the Great Migration from Mexico to the United States between 1900 and 1930?

FQ 2: How did the Porfiriato and the Mexican Revolution displace people, reshape political identities, and accelerate migration northward?

IN MY WORDS: What pushed people out of Mexico, and what pulled them to the United States?

READING A | THE PORFIRIATO: PROGRESS FOR WHOM?

Between 1900 and 1930, more than one million Mexicans moved to the United States. Historians call this the Great Migration. Many entered without official papers, because U.S. officials rarely enforced the rules.

The story begins with Porfirio Díaz, who ruled Mexico as a dictator from 1876 to 1911. Díaz promised “order and progress.” He invited foreign investors, especially British and American, to build railroads, mines, and oil wells. By 1910 Mexico had about 12,000 miles of railroad. The economy grew, but mostly for the rich and for foreigners.

Meanwhile, Mexico's population jumped from 8.7 million to 15 million. Indigenous villages lost their ejidos, shared lands, which were broken up and handed to wealthy Mexicans and foreigners. One Chihuahua landowner, Luis Terrazas, held almost five million acres. By 1910 only 3 percent of Mexicans owned any land at all. Most country people became peons working on haciendas. Most historians see this land crisis as the main cause of the coming revolution.

Word help: dictator = ruler with total power · ejido = village land shared by a community · peon = poor farm worker tied to an estate · hacienda = large estate

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 5, “Motives for Mexican Immigration” and “The Mexican Revolution,” pp. 129–133.

Part 1 continued · Revolution and Exile

READING B | REVOLUTION, WAR, AND EXILE

In November 1910, Francisco Madero launched a revolt against Díaz. It grew into a full social revolution. Leaders fought for power, and many, including Madero, Zapata, Carranza, and Villa, were killed. Between 1.5 and 2 million people died. The worst fighting hit the crowded Bajío region (Guanajuato, Michoacán, Jalisco), where destroyed crops left people near starvation. In a single day in October 1913, about 8,000 refugees crossed into Eagle Pass, Texas.

Peace did not fix everything. Land reform moved slowly. From 1926 to 1929, the Cristero Rebellion broke out in western Mexico when Catholics fought the government's anti-Church laws. About 80,000 died, and as many as half a million refugees may have reached the United States. The 1920s saw the largest exodus of all.

Some migrants were political exiles from the middle and upper classes. Many settled in San Antonio's West Side. They sold Mexican books, newspapers, and records, sponsored plays and speeches, formed their own clubs, and kept Mexican patriotism alive.

Word help: revolution = complete change of a society, often through war · refugee = person fleeing danger · anticlerical = against the power of the Church · exile = person forced to leave home

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 5, “The Mexican Revolution,” pp. 133–137.

READING C | PULLED NORTH: THE MINES

Mexicans chose the Southwest for many reasons. It had been part of Mexico, the land and climate felt familiar, and railroads ran straight from central Mexico to El Paso and Laredo. Relatives who had already moved often sent money and advice, a pattern called chain migration. Above all, there were jobs. Texas's Mexican-origin population grew from about 71,000 in 1900 to about 684,000 in 1930.

Mining offered the first big opportunity. After the Apache wars ended, copper and silver mining boomed in Arizona and New Mexico, but there were too few workers. Sonora, just across the border, had experienced miners, and Americans owned mines on both sides. By the mid-1920s, Mexicanos made up 43 percent of Arizona's copper miners.

The work was dangerous. Under the dual-wage system, Mexicano miners earned less than white miners for the same job. They were segregated, lived in poor company housing, and paid high prices at company stores.

Word help: chain migration = moving to join family who went first · dual-wage system = two pay scales, lower for Mexicans · company town = town owned by the employer

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 5, “The Economic Development of the Southwest,” pp. 137–138.

Part 1 continued · Railroads, Fields, and the Bigger Picture

READING D | RAILROADS AND FIELDS

Railroads also needed Mexican workers. European immigrants who built the lines in the 1870s and 1880s moved on to better jobs, and track repair needed no special skills. From 1900 to 1940, Mexicans made up 60 to 90 percent of the track crews on western railroads. Many lived in boxcars or labor camps along the line, and some were sent as far as Chicago.

Farming became the biggest magnet. The Newlands Reclamation Act of 1902 paid for irrigation projects that turned desert into farmland. Big corporate “factory farms” needed huge numbers of workers. Other sources of labor were shrinking: the Chinese Exclusion Act and an agreement with Japan cut Asian immigration, and the quota laws of 1921 and 1924 limited Europeans. By the 1920s, Mexicanos did most farm work in the Southwest. Cotton employed the most, since whole families could pick it. The pay was the lowest of any job in the country.

Word help: irrigation = bringing water to dry land · quota = a set limit on numbers · factory farm = huge farm run like a business

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 5, “The Economic Development of the Southwest,” pp. 138–141.

READING E | A BIGGER PICTURE: U.S. MONEY IN MEXICO

Historians Gilbert González and Raúl Fernández argue that push and pull were both part of something larger. Starting in the 1870s, U.S. companies poured money into Mexico. By 1896, railroads made up 70 percent of all U.S. investment there, and Americans also bought mines, cattle ranches, and irrigated cotton farms in northern Mexico.

This investment changed Mexican life. Foreign competition pushed many peasants off their land. U.S. railroad, mining, and farm companies then hired them to work in the thinly settled northern states. Once workers had moved north once, crossing the border into the United States was often the next step.

Word help: investment = money put into a business to make profit · peasant = small farmer or farm worker · globalization = economies around the world becoming linked

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 5, “US Imperialism and the Mexican Exodus,” pp. 141–142.

READING F | ONE IMMIGRANT'S STORY

Gonzales builds a composite (combined) life from many real stories. “José Martínez” grew up poor in a village in western Mexico and fought in the Revolution. Afterward, poverty was as bad as ever and land reform disappointed him, so in 1923 he borrowed money for a train ticket north. At El Paso he crossed easily: a literacy test and an $8 head tax existed, but they were barely enforced because employers wanted cheap labor.

José worked on railroad crews and in coal mines, then sent for his wife, María. The family became migrant farmworkers in Texas. Saving money took far longer than planned, their children were born in the United States, and they gave up the idea of going home.

Word help: composite = made by combining many examples · literacy test = reading test · head tax = fee charged per person entering · migrant = moving from place to place for work

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 5, “The Immigrant,” pp. 142–147.

SOURCE A | A MEXICANA ON THE RAILROAD

Manuela Oaxaca Quinn fought as a soldadera in the Revolution, then moved with her family from one railroad or mining camp to another and even repaired track beside her husband. Like many poor Mexicanas, she worked outside the home because the family needed the money. Her son, actor Anthony Quinn, recorded her memories.

“It might even sound strange, a young girl working on the railroad, but we Mexican women were used to it. We fought beside our men and found it only normal to work beside them.”

Manuela Oaxaca Quinn, as remembered in Anthony Quinn, The Original Sin (1972); quoted in Gonzales, Mexicanos, p. 146.

READING G | THE CONTRATISTA

Most newcomers spoke little English and needed one thing above all: work. Contratistas, labor contractors who were often Mexican Americans, connected workers with growers and companies. Some cared about their crews, but their power invited abuse.

A grower usually paid the contratista a lump sum to harvest a crop, so the less he paid workers, the more he kept. Tricks included switching from hourly pay to piece rates when the crop thinned, and holding back part of the pay (called los bonos) until season's end, which workers lost if they left early. Some cheated on the scale when weighing cotton. Many sold food at high prices, paid in scrip good only at their own store, or charged for “favors.” A few even ran off to Mexico with the payroll.

Some contratistas became rich, but most lost their fortunes, so the system rarely led to lasting success for anyone.

Word help: lump sum = one payment for the whole job · piece rate = pay per amount picked · scrip = paper money usable only at one store · exploitation = unfair use of someone's work

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 5, “The Contratista,” pp. 147–149.

Part 2 continued · Camp Life

READING H | LIFE IN THE CAMPS

Most Mexicanos in the 1920s lived in the countryside, at least part of the year, and moved constantly between farm, mine, and railroad jobs. Migrant families usually lived in tents or one-room shacks near the fields. Sanitation was poor, insects and rats were common, and diseases like tuberculosis spread. Children could rarely stay in school.

Workers seldom protested. Many did not speak English or know their rights, and those who complained could be blacklisted or deported. Many also compared their lives with the poverty they had left and expected to return to Mexico soon.

Camps also brought different groups together. In California's Imperial Valley, Punjabi men from India often married Mexican women. Laws banned many mixed marriages, but Mexicans' unclear racial status let these marriages happen. Their children later tended to call themselves Indian rather than Mexican, because anti-Mexican prejudice was so strong.

Word help: transient = moving often · sanitation = clean water and waste removal · blacklist = list of workers no one will hire · anti-miscegenation law = law banning marriage across races

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 5, “The Immigrant” and “Rural Life,” pp. 145–151.

READING I | BEYOND THE SOUTHWEST, AND A COMPARISON

Sugar beets drew Mexicanos to Colorado around 1900 and later to the Midwest; by 1927 about 58,000 worked in beet fields. Migrants planned each trip with family and friends who had gone before. By the 1930s, Detroit and Chicago had large barrios.

Mexican and European immigrants both relied on family and mutual-aid societies, but the differences mattered more. Most Europeans came to stay; Mexico was close, so many Mexicans kept ties and crossed back and forth. Europeans found factory jobs in Eastern cities; Mexicans did mining, railroad, and farm work that kept them moving. Europeans entered legally through centers like Ellis Island; most Mexicans came without papers, which became a crime in 1929. Finally, Europeans were treated as white, while Mexicans were seen as nonwhite and faced harsher racism.

Word help: colonia = Mexican settlement · betabelero = sugar beet worker · assimilation = becoming part of the larger society

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 5, “Mexicanos Beyond the Southwest” and “Mexican and European Immigration,” pp. 151–155.
