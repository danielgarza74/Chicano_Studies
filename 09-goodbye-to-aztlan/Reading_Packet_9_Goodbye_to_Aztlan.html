New Numbers, New Arrivals

CORE OBJECTIVES

FQ 1: How did population growth, youth, and geographic dispersal transform the national profile of Mexicanos after 1975?

FQ 2: What forces drove renewed Mexican immigration after 1965, and how did immigration policy shape the experiences of newcomers?

IN MY WORDS: How did more births, more immigrants, and new places to live change Mexicano communities?

Reading aA National Minority

After 1975 the Mexican-origin population of the United States grew very quickly. The Census Bureau's “Hispanic” group, which included Mexicanos, Puerto Ricans, and Cubans, grew 61 percent in the 1970s and 53 percent in the 1980s. By 1990 it numbered 22.4 million, and about 13.5 million were of Mexican origin. Families were larger (3.8 people, compared with 3.2 for a typical American family), and the community was young: its median age was 26.2, while the nation's was 33.0.

Mexicanos were also moving to new places. By the 1990s they picked citrus in Florida, harvested tobacco in North Carolina, and worked in Iowa slaughterhouses. About 200,000 lived in New York City, and over 1.5 million lived around Chicago and Detroit. Still, 83 percent remained in the Southwest.

Most lived in cities: 90.5 percent by 1990. Greater Los Angeles held about 4.8 million Latinos in 1992. Its Mexicano community was the fourth largest in the world, behind three cities in Mexico.

Word help: census = official count of the people · median age = the middle age (half are older, half younger) · urban = living in cities

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 9, “Demographic Trends,” pp. 284–285.

Reading BWhy So Many Came

Immigration from Mexico climbed in the 1970s and soared in the 1980s. The main reason was simple: Mexico could not give many of its people a decent living. Its population grew by more than 30 million in twenty years. In 1981–1982 the economy crashed, hit by falling oil prices, high inflation, and a foreign debt it could not pay. Then in December 1994 the peso collapsed, and living standards for most Mexicans dropped about 20 percent.

This wave looked different from earlier ones. More migrants came from cities, including professionals who had lost hope at home. Indigenous people, such as Mixtecos and Oaxacans, arrived in large numbers. Women and whole families were more common, and refugees from wars in Central America came as well.

The jobs had changed, too. Farm and railroad work was harder to find. Most openings were low-wage service jobs: restaurants, nurseries, and yard work for men, and child care and housework for women.

Word help: peso = Mexico's money · inflation = rising prices · indigenous = Native · service jobs = work serving customers, not making goods

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 9, “The Resurgence of Mexican Immigration,” pp. 285–289.

Reading CThe Immigration Debate

Rising immigration set off a bitter national argument, mostly about money. Defenders said immigrants did work that citizens would not do; in the early 1990s more than 92 percent of California's farmworkers were foreign-born. A 1994 Urban Institute study found that immigrants paid more in taxes than they used in services. Civil rights groups said immigrants were being blamed for problems they did not cause.

Critics disagreed. They argued that immigrants cost more in schools, health care, and welfare than their work was worth, and that many did not pay their fair share of taxes. Some critics, including members of the Federation for American Immigration Reform (FAIR), spread racial theories.

Mexican Americans were divided too. A 1992 survey found that most Mexicanos, citizens and noncitizens, felt there were too many immigrants. Recent arrivals who competed for the same jobs were the most opposed. Yet many feared that a backlash, such as a national ID card, would bring discrimination against every Latino.

Word help: scapegoat = someone blamed for others' problems · restriction = limiting · backlash = an angry reaction against a group

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 9, “The Resurgence of Mexican Immigration,” pp. 289–291.

Reading DIrca, 1986

By the mid-1980s anger over immigration was so strong in parts of California that Congress had to act. The hardest fight was over punishing employers, which many Republicans opposed. The compromise was the Immigration Reform and Control Act (IRCA), signed in 1986.

IRCA tried to solve the problem in two ways. First, it set penalties for employers who hired undocumented workers. Second, it offered amnesty: legal status and a path to citizenship for undocumented people who had lived in the country since January 1, 1982, and for farmworkers who had worked at least 90 days in 1985–1986. About 2.4 million people gained legal status.

The law slowed illegal entry for a few years, but by 1990 crossings were rising again. Fake documents were easy to buy, and the government barely enforced the employer penalties. Fewer than 350 agents watched seven million employers, and fewer than half of reported cases ended in fines.

Word help: amnesty = official pardon · sanctions = penalties · enforce = make people obey a law

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 9, “The Resurgence of Mexican Immigration,” pp. 291–292.

Free Trade and a Promise Unfulfilled

CORE OBJECTIVES

FQ 3: How was NAFTA expected to address migration and economic development, and why did Mexican American communities disagree about it?

FQ 4: Why did the proclaimed Decade of the Hispanic fail to fulfill its political and socioeconomic promise?

IN MY WORDS: Why did leaders hope trade would help, and why did the 1980s disappoint many Mexicanos?

Reading ENafta: a New Answer?

When IRCA failed, some leaders tried a different idea: if Mexico's economy grew stronger, fewer Mexicans would need to leave. Since 1965, U.S. companies had built assembly plants called maquiladoras just south of the border to use cheap labor. Workers, mostly women, assembled parts for appliances, cars, and other goods sold in the United States. The plants also encouraged more movement back and forth across the border.

Over time, leaders of both U.S. parties backed a free-trade zone. They hoped it would give Mexicans jobs at home and, above all, open Mexico's markets to American business. Mexico's government hoped it would modernize its economy. The result was NAFTA, a trade pact among Canada, Mexico, and the United States that took effect on January 1, 1994.

Mexican American communities disagreed about it. Rep. Bill Richardson of New Mexico helped pass it, and Latino business leaders and the Southwest Voter Registration and Education Project supported it. Labor unions and environmental groups, led by Ross Perot, fought it.

Word help: maquiladora = assembly plant in Mexico owned by a foreign company · free trade = trade without taxes on imports · modernize = update

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 9, “The North American Free Trade Association,” pp. 292–293.

Reading FThe “Decade of the Hispanic”

Many Mexicanos expected the 1980s to bring big gains. Raúl Yzaguirre of the National Council of La Raza and others called it the “Decade of the Hispanic.” A few leaders won major offices, such as Toney Anaya, elected governor of New Mexico in 1982. Overall, though, progress was slow.

Politics was one problem. In most elections fewer than one in four eligible Mexicanos voted. Organizations often refused to cooperate, and Mexicanos, mostly Democrats, found little common ground with Republican Cuban Americans. The national mood also turned conservative. Republicans held power most of the period, and critics of social programs, such as Richard Rodríguez and Linda Chávez, came from inside the community. The English Only movement spread, and in 1996 California voters passed Proposition 209, ending affirmative action.

Schools and poverty held people back. The high school dropout rate was over 40 percent in the early 1990s. In 1991 the median Hispanic family earned $23,431, compared with $35,353 for all families; 28.1 percent of Mexicanos were poor, and only about 20 percent had health insurance.

Word help: turnout = share of voters who vote · conservative = favoring tradition and less government help · affirmative action = programs to open schools and jobs to minorities

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 9, “The Decade of the Hispanic: The Unfulfilled Promise,” pp. 293–297.

Faith, Feministas, and a Renaissance

CORE OBJECTIVES

FQ 5: How did changing Catholic, Protestant, and secular influences shape Mexicano identity and community life?

FQ 6: How did second-generation feministas expand the agenda of Chicana activism through professional, political, and community organizations?

IN MY WORDS: How did churches and Chicana activists shape Mexicano communities?

Reading GFaith in the Barrio

For a long time the Catholic Church did little for Mexicano parishioners. Its leaders, many of them Irish, did not understand Mexican folk traditions and sometimes treated Mexicanos almost like pagans. Few Mexicanos became priests, and some parishes were still segregated in the 1950s.

Beginning in the 1960s, the Church reached out. The Cursillo, a three-day program of Bible study and prayer, trained Latino lay leaders; César Chávez was one of them. Meetings called encuentros (1972, 1977, 1985) tried to bridge the gap between Spanish-speaking and Anglo Catholics. By 1983 there were fifteen Hispanic bishops. In San Antonio, Archbishop Patrick Flores backed social action and the community group COPS.

Many still left. Protestant and Pentecostal churches offered a more personal faith, conservative family values, and small congregations led by Spanish-speaking ministers. By 2006, 12 percent of Mexicanos were Pentecostal. A 2007 Pew study found only about 74 percent still Catholic, and a growing number belonged to no church at all, a sign of blending into mainstream American life.

Word help: parish = local church community · lay leader = church member who is not a priest · Pentecostal = Protestant church stressing the Holy Spirit · secular = not religious

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 9, “The Quest for Cristo Rey,” pp. 302–307.

Reading HChicanas Lead

From the mid-1970s to the mid-1990s, Mexicanas made real gains. More went to college; in 1991 Latinas earned more bachelor's degrees than Latino men. They also rose in politics. Gloria Molina became the first Chicana elected to the California State Assembly (1982) and in 1991 the first woman of any background elected to the Los Angeles County Board of Supervisors. In 1992 Lucille Roybal-Allard became the first Mexicana elected to Congress.

Some of the most effective groups grew in the barrio. Mothers of East Los Angeles, a Catholic women's group led by Juana Beatriz Gutiérrez, campaigned for years and stopped the state from building a prison in their neighborhood.

On campuses and in professions, Chicanas built their own networks. In 1982 they founded MALCS, a support and research network for Chicana professors. Inside the National Association for Chicano Studies, a women's caucus pushed until the group added “Chicana” to its name in 1994. Nationally, the Mexican American Women's National Association (MANA) became a leading voice.

Word help: feminista = activist for women's rights · caucus = group inside a larger organization · network = group that supports its members

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 9, “Feministas: The Second Generation,” pp. 307–310.

A Chicano Renaissance

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 7: How did the Chicano Renaissance in scholarship, literature, and the arts preserve and redefine Mexican American identity beyond Aztlán?

IN MY WORDS: How did scholars, writers, and artists keep Mexican American culture alive and change how it was seen?

Reading IA Chicano Renaissance

After the 1960s, Mexican Americans created so much literature and art that the era is called the Chicano Renaissance. Scholars also rediscovered forgotten authors such as María Amparo Ruiz de Burton, a nineteenth-century novelist, folklorist Jovita González, and novelist Josefina Niggli. In 1992 Arte Público Press launched the Recovering the U.S. Hispanic Literary Heritage project.

New writers explored identity, migrant work, and family life. Tomás Rivera's ...y no se lo tragó la tierra (1971) told of Texas migrant workers. Rudolfo Anaya's Bless Me Ultima became a best seller, and Sandra Cisneros wrote The House on Mango Street (1984), a coming-of-age story. Luis Valdez founded El Teatro Campesino to support farmworkers and later created Zoot Suit; Edward James Olmos starred in Stand and Deliver.

Muralists painted the Virgen de Guadalupe, Indigenous heritage, and the Mexican Revolution; Judith Baca led the movement in Los Angeles. In music, Los Tigres del Norte became the voice of immigrants, and Selena was crossing over to pop when she was killed in 1995.

Word help: renaissance = rebirth of art and learning · folklorist = studier of traditional stories · mural = wall painting

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 9, “The Chicano Renaissance,” pp. 310–322.
