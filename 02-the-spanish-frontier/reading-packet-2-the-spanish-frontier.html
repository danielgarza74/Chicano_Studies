Part 1 · Exploring the Far North

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 1: How did the search for God, gold, and glory shape Spanish exploration of the Far North, and why did exploration not immediately produce permanent settlement?

IN MY WORDS: What were Spanish explorers looking for in the north, and why did they not stay?

READING A | SEARCHING FOR THE SEVEN CITIES

After conquering the Aztecs, Spaniards spread out seeking God, gold, and glory. The first to cross the Far North came by accident. In 1528 a storm wrecked an expedition off Texas, and only four men lived: Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca, two other Spaniards, and an African Moor named Estevánico. Cabeza de Vaca kept them alive by working as a healer among Native tribes. Back in Mexico in 1536, their stories grew into rumors of the golden Seven Cities of Cíbola.

In 1539 Fray Marcos de Niza went north and claimed he saw the cities. In 1540 Francisco Vásquez de Coronado led about 1,100 people after them. Cíbola turned out to be plain Zuñi villages. Coronado chased another rumored kingdom, Gran Quivira, as far as Kansas and came home in disgrace in 1542. That year Juan Rodríguez Cabrillo sailed up the Pacific coast past San Diego Bay. His crew found good land and a mild climate, but no gold.

Word help: expedition = an organized journey · rumor = a story that may not be true · disgrace = shame

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 2, “Spanish Exploration of the Far North,” pp. 34–36.

READING C | OÑATE'S COLONY IN NEW MEXICO

Spain finally settled New Mexico in the 1590s mainly out of fear that England, whose captain Francis Drake had raided Spanish ships and landed in California in 1579, might claim the north.

In 1598, Juan de Oñate, the son of a rich silver-mine owner, led about 130 soldiers and their families up the Camino Real, the long road from Mexico City. They settled beside the Pueblo towns on the upper Rio Grande. Spaniards saw the Pueblo farmers as future Christians and workers. Oñate's men received encomiendas, the right to use Native labor in return for “protecting” and teaching them. Santa Fe was founded around 1610.

After the people of Ácoma ambushed a Spanish party in 1598, Oñate's troops killed about 800 of them. Survivors over age twelve were sentenced to twenty years of slavery, and men over twenty-one also had a foot cut off.

Word help: rival = competitor · encomienda = a grant of the right to Native people's labor · ambush = a surprise attack

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 2, “Settlement of New Mexico,” pp. 37–40.

Part 1 continued · The Pueblo Revolt

READING D | MISSIONS AND THE PUEBLO REVOLT

Wherever Spain went, it built two institutions: missions, where friars taught Catholicism and Spanish, and presidios, forts that protected settlers. In New Mexico, Franciscan friars worked among the Pueblos. Settlers demanded as much Pueblo labor as they could get, while the friars tried to wipe out Pueblo religion with harsh methods. Of all these pressures, the attacks on their faith angered the Pueblos most.

A long drought made life even harder. On August 10, 1680, a religious leader named Popé led a huge surprise uprising. About 400 settlers and most of the 33 friars were killed, and the survivors fled south to El Paso. It was one of the few times a European colony was forced to retreat.

Diego de Vargas retook Santa Fe in 1692. Afterward Spain changed course: it ended the encomienda and stopped the worst abuses, and relations with the Pueblos improved in the 1700s.

Word help: friar = a member of a Catholic religious order · presidio = a frontier fort · drought = a long time without rain

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 2, “Settlement of New Mexico,” pp. 41–43.

Part 2 · Expanding the Frontier

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 3: Why did Spain extend settlement beyond New Mexico into Texas, Arizona, and California, and how did Indigenous resistance and European rivalry shape those frontier communities?

IN MY WORDS: Why did Spain settle Texas, Arizona, and California, and who made that hard?

READING E | TEXAS AND ARIZONA

In 1682 the French explorer La Salle claimed the Mississippi Valley for France. Afraid that France would push west, Spain sent missions and soldiers into Texas. In 1718 it founded a mission and presidio at San Antonio, and in 1731 families from the Canary Islands started a town there. Still, Texas stayed small. Comanche warriors on horseback ruled the plains, and by 1800 Texas had only about 3,500 Tejanos.

Arizona was settled mainly for religion. Jesuit priests built missions up Mexico's west coast. The most famous, Father Eusebio Kino, founded twenty-four missions from Sinaloa to southern Arizona, including San Xavier del Bac in 1700. Apache raids and too few priests slowed the work, and in 1751 the Pimas rebelled and killed about 100 settlers.

In 1776 Spain joined its northern provinces under one military command to fight raids better. Ranching, trade, and population grew after that.

Word help: Tejano = a Spanish-speaking resident of Texas · Jesuit = a member of a Catholic religious order · rebel = rise up against rulers

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 2, “Settlement of the Northern Frontier: Beyond New Mexico,” pp. 43–49.

Part 2 continued · Alta California

READING F | ALTA CALIFORNIA

Spain moved into Alta California in 1769. The royal inspector José de Gálvez pushed the plan, partly to block the Russians and partly to boost his own career. Captain Gaspar de Portolá and the Franciscan friar Junípero Serra led the expedition. Serra founded the first mission at San Diego in 1769, and in time twenty-one missions stretched up the coast, guarded by four presidios.

California had more than 300,000 Native people in 1769, one of the largest populations in North America. Coastal Indians were pulled into mission life with surprising ease. Neophytes (converts) learned farming, ranching, and crafts, but they lived in conditions close to slavery, and European diseases spread fast. The coastal Indian population fell from about 60,000 in 1769 to about 35,000 in 1800.

Few settlers came. Juan Bautista de Anza opened a land trail from Arizona, but in 1781 a Yuma uprising killed 104 people and closed it. By 1800, only about 1,200 non-Indians lived in California.

Word help: neophyte = a new convert living at a mission · Russians = traders moving down the Pacific coast · uprising = a revolt

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 2, “Settlement of the Northern Frontier: Beyond New Mexico,” pp. 49–52.

Part 2 continued · What Made the Borderlands Different?

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 4: What demographic, racial, social, and economic patterns defined the Spanish Borderlands, and how did they distinguish this frontier from the later Anglo-American frontier?

IN MY WORDS: What was life like on the Spanish frontier, and how was it different from the Anglo-American frontier?

READING G | LIFE ON THE FRONTIER

By 1800, Spain's Far North looked like three fingers reaching north: California on the coast, New Mexico in the center, and Texas in the east. Almost no settlers lived in between, and the three areas were cut off from each other. New Mexico was the largest, with about 40,000 people in 1821.

Life was hard and short; most people lived only to about forty. Raids, disease, and childbirth took many lives, and widows were common. Frontier women had more freedom than women in central Mexico. In New Mexico they ran businesses, owned property, sued in court, and could even divorce.

Society was also more open. The old ranking by race and class broke down, and land was easy to get, so most settlers owned some. Ricos (the rich) and pobres (the poor) lived in similar ways, and families were tied together by marriage and godparenthood.

Word help: isolated = cut off from others · widow = a woman whose husband has died · ricos and pobres = rich and poor

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 2, “Major Trends,” pp. 52–55.

Part 2 continued · A Mixed People

READING H | A MIXED PEOPLE

Mestizaje, the mixing of peoples, shaped the whole frontier. Official records often called settlers español (Spanish), but the label went to almost anyone who was not plainly Indian or African and who lived in a Spanish way. It described culture more than race.

The mixing happened in many ways. Mexican Indians such as Tlaxcalans came north with Spanish settlers. In New Mexico, settlers married Pueblos and genízaros, Native captives ransomed from nomadic tribes, and a 1776 census counted most New Mexicans as mestizos. Most Texas settlers were already mestizos from northern Mexico, and some had African ancestry. In California, nearly all the men who came with Portolá and Serra were Indians or of mixed blood. Of the 23 colonists who founded Los Angeles in 1781, only two were white.

Word help: mestizaje = the mixing of peoples · census = an official count of people · ransomed = bought back from captors

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 2, “Major Trends,” pp. 56–59.

Part 2 continued · Two Frontiers Compared

READING I | TWO FRONTIERS

In 1893, historian Frederick Jackson Turner argued that English-speaking settlers moving west onto “free land” created the American character and American democracy. His Frontier Thesis became the most popular way to explain U.S. history. Chicana/o historians later criticized it for ignoring the racism of westward expansion and for leaving out the Spanish frontier in the Southwest.

The two frontiers really were different. On Spain's frontier, religion mattered most and the mission was the key institution; on the Anglo-American frontier, missions played a small role. Spaniards exploited Indians, sometimes brutally, but many saw them as human beings with souls, and intermarriage was common. Few Anglo-American frontiersmen saw Indians that way, and Native peoples were eventually pushed onto reservations or killed. Finally, Spain's expansion came mainly from government plans, and frontier life was tightly regulated, while the Anglo-American West grew more from individual initiative.

Word help: thesis = a main argument · institution = an important organization, like a church or fort · initiative = acting on your own

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 2, introduction and “Major Trends,” pp. 33–34, 61–62.
