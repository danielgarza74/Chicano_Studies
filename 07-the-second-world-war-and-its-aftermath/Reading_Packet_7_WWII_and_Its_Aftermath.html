Serving the Nation

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 1: How did military service and wartime work transform Mexican American identity, expectations, and demands for civil rights?

IN MY WORDS: How did fighting and working in the war change what Mexican Americans expected from the United States?

Reading AMexicanos Go to War

Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, pulled the United States into World War II. Of roughly 2.7 million Mexicanos in the country, somewhere between a quarter million and half a million served. Most were drafted, but many volunteered, probably at a higher rate than other Americans.

Why sign up? For young men stuck in poor barrios or small towns, the military was a fast way to escape poverty and learn job skills. Many immigrants felt grateful to their new country and joined out of patriotism. Some saw service as a way to prove they were men.

Mexicanos fought in the Pacific, North Africa, and Europe and won many medals. Twelve Hispanic soldiers, apparently all Mexicanos, earned the Medal of Honor in this war. Sergeant José M. López of Brownsville, Texas, killed more than 100 German soldiers in Belgium in December 1944. Marine Guy Gabaldón of East Los Angeles, who spoke Japanese, captured more than 1,500 enemy soldiers and civilians on Saipan in 1944.

Word help: drafted = required by law to serve · enlist = sign up · patriotism = love of country · Medal of Honor = the highest U.S. military award

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 7, “Mexicans in the Military,” pp. 209–212.

Reading BComing Home and the Home Front

War changed how many Mexicano soldiers saw themselves. Fighting side by side with Anglos often broke down prejudice, and veterans came home with confidence and new skills. The G.I. Bill, signed in 1944, paid for the schooling of 7.8 million returning soldiers. It also helped veterans get job training and buy homes. Several future Mexican American scholars and writers went to college this way.

Women did their part too. Some Hispanas served in uniform, but most mujeres worked on the home front. They sold war bonds and formed groups to support the troops, such as Tucson's Club de Madres y Esposas (Club of Mothers and Wives). With men away, women filled jobs in the fields, in food-processing plants, and sometimes in defense plants, which a 1941 executive order opened to minority workers. Overall, the war raised the standard of living for both men and women.

Word help: veteran = a person who served in the military · war bonds = loans people made to the government to pay for the war · defense plant = factory making war supplies

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 7, “Mexicans in the Military,” pp. 212–214.

Source AA Veteran Remembers

Raúl Morín of Lockhart, Texas, fought in World War II and later wrote a history of Mexican American soldiers. Here he describes how other soldiers treated Mexicanos by the end of the war (compare Reading B).

“No longer were we chided and shunned by other GI's and Army officers. Where we had been held in contempt by others . . . we were now admired, respected, and approved by all those around us including most of our commanding officers.”

Raúl Morín, Among the Valiant: Mexican-Americans in WWII and Korea (1966); quoted in Gonzales, Mexicanos, “Mexicans in the Military,” p. 212. Veteran's account (primary).

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 2: How did wartime urbanization create new opportunities while also producing conflict, discrimination, and the Zoot Suit violence?

IN MY WORDS: Why did moving to the city bring both new chances and new dangers?

Reading CMoving to the City

The best wartime jobs were in cities, so Mexicanos poured into border cities, Chicago, Detroit, Portland, and Seattle. Living standards slowly rose, and some families left the barrio and met Anglos as neighbors, not just bosses.

But discrimination stayed. Newcomers faced poor housing, few good jobs, and segregated schools. City life also strained the family. On a farm, a father expected obedience; in the city, his wife and children met a culture that questioned old customs. Women who earned paychecks demanded more rights and usually won them, but the fast change often caused conflict at home, sometimes including domestic violence.

Word help: urban = in a city · barrio = Mexicano neighborhood · segregated = kept separate by race · standard of living = how well people are able to live

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 7, “Urbanization: Trials and Tribulations,” pp. 214–215.

Reading DPachucos and the Sleepy Lagoon Case

In the early 1940s some Mexicano teens, called pachucos and pachucas or “zoot-suiters,” created their own style: baggy suits, big hats, and a slang called caló. Their immigrant parents' Old World ways did not fit them, and Anglos shut them out of jobs and schools. Caught between two worlds, they built their own.

In August 1942, José Díaz was found dead near the Sleepy Lagoon, east of Los Angeles. On only circumstantial evidence, 22 members of the 38th Street gang were charged with murder. The press stirred up fear, Judge Charles Fricke would not let the youths clean up for court, and no Mexicanos sat on the jury. Seventeen were convicted. Mexicanos saw racism in the trial, and a defense committee won a reversal in 1944.

Word help: circumstantial = indirect, not proof · reservoir = a small lake that stores water · overturned = canceled by a higher court

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 7, “Urbanization: Trials and Tribulations,” pp. 215–218.

Reading EThe Zoot Suit Riots, 1943

Southern California was full of military training camps, and servicemen and pachucos clashed often. On June 3, 1943, after a minor fight, sailors and marines on leave stormed into the barrios of Los Angeles. They beat Mexicano youths, girls as well as boys, many of whom had nothing to do with pachucos. Newspapers blamed the Mexicanos, and police mostly looked away. The attacks spread to other places and went on for about a week.

The violence finally stopped when President Franklin Roosevelt had the military cancel passes and order the men back to their bases. A major reason he acted was a strong protest from Mexico, a wartime ally, because some victims were Mexican citizens. Many Mexican Americans wondered how they could be attacked at home while their sons were fighting overseas.

Word help: servicemen = members of the military · on leave = off duty · ally = a partner in war

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 7, “Urbanization: Trials and Tribulations,” pp. 218–219.

Wanted as Workers, Pushed Out as Immigrants?

CORE OBJECTIVES

FQ 3: Why was the Bracero Program created, how did it operate, and what were its consequences for growers, braceros, and domestic workers?

FQ 4: How did Operation Wetback expose the contradiction between U.S. demand for Mexican labor and campaigns against undocumented immigration?

IN MY WORDS: How did the United States both invite and expel Mexican workers?

Reading FThe Bracero Program Begins

The war left Southwest farms badly short of workers. Farming was booming, farmworkers were leaving for city jobs, and after Pearl Harbor no workers could come from Asia. On July 23, 1942, President Roosevelt and Mexico's President Manuel Ávila Camacho agreed to bring in braceros: Mexican men hired to work in the United States for a limited time. In different forms, the program lasted until December 1964.

Mexico recruited men using a quota for each Mexican state, often by lottery, because far more men wanted to go than were needed. Chosen men went to screening centers in northern Mexico for record checks and medical exams. Then they crossed to U.S. centers, such as El Centro and El Paso, where employers made the final choice. Each worker signed a contract. The farmer provided housing, transportation, and work; the bracero paid for food and health insurance out of his wages.

Word help: bracero = a Mexican contract farmworker · quota = a set number · screening = checking people before choosing them

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 7, “The Bracero Program,” pp. 219–222.

Reading GWho Gained and Who Lost?

Braceros' wages sent home helped Mexico, but camp life was hard. Pay was low, housing was often poor, and the food was cheap and unfamiliar. Discrimination was severe, and contract protections were often ignored. In 1963 near Salinas, California, a train struck a crowded bus and killed 32 braceros.

The biggest winners were large growers in Texas and California. Braceros came only to earn money, so they worked hard, seldom missed work, almost never went on strike, and stayed through the whole harvest. The government, not the grower, paid for recruiting them. On paper, U.S.-born farmworkers were protected, but in practice braceros often took their jobs and pulled farm wages down. Unions, small farmers, and anti-immigrant groups opposed it, and as machines took over more of the harvest, Congress let it end in 1964.

Word help: grower = owner of a large farm · harvest = gathering of crops · domestic workers = here, U.S.-born workers

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 7, “The Bracero Program,” pp. 222–224.

Reading HUndocumented Workers and Operation Wetback

Illegal crossings grew after the war. Far more men wanted to work in the north than the Bracero Program could take. In Mexico, the population doubled from 1934 to 1958 and wages fell behind prices, while border cities like Tijuana grew fast. The border was nearly 2,000 miles long, and the Border Patrol was kept small. Many growers welcomed undocumented workers, who were even cheaper than braceros.

Cold War fears made many Americans anxious about immigration. On June 17, 1954, the government began “Operation Wetback,” a military-style Border Patrol campaign that often used intimidation. In 1954, 1,075,168 people were caught and sent to Mexico. (“Wetback” and “mojado” are insulting terms; the government used the word in the operation's name.)

Word help: undocumented = without legal papers to enter or stay · apprehended = caught · intimidation = scaring people into obeying

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 7, “Operation Wetback,” pp. 224–226.

Julián Samora, a sociologist, explains why some growers hired undocumented workers instead of braceros (1971; secondary source).

“Wetback labor is even cheaper [than bracero labor]—no need for contracts, minimum wage, health benefits, housing, transportation, etc. Since the workers are illegal aliens, they have few rights before the law and can be dismissed at a moment's notice.”

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 5: How did the Mexican American Generation use citizenship, military service, middle-class organizations, and legal activism to seek inclusion on its own terms?

IN MY WORDS: How did Mexican American groups fight for equal rights as U.S. citizens?

Reading ICitizens Organize: Lulac and the Courts

U.S.-born Mexican Americans of the war years, called the Mexican American Generation, saw this country as home and used citizenship, organizations, and the courts to fight discrimination.

The League of United Latin American Citizens (LULAC) began in Corpus Christi, Texas, in 1929. Only U.S. citizens could join, so most members were middle class. LULAC used English and taught American values, but it promised to defend the whole Mexicano community from discrimination.

School segregation was a major target. In 1945, five families in Orange County, California, led by Gonzalo and Felícitas Méndez, sued to end separate “Mexican” schools. Méndez v. Westminster (1947) ended legal school segregation in California. LULAC led the fight in Delgado v. Bastrop (1948), which did the same for Texas. Both cases helped prepare the way for Brown v. Board of Education (1954).

Word help: citizen = a legal member of a nation · segregation = separating people by race · lawsuit = a case taken to court

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 7, “The Mexican American Generation,” pp. 227–230.

Reading JVeterans and Voters

In East Los Angeles, the Community Service Organization (CSO) grew out of Edward Roybal's 1947 city council race. Organizer Fred Ross trained members to register voters, and in 1949 Roybal won, the first Mexicano on the council since 1881.

In 1948, Dr. Héctor P. García founded the American G.I. Forum in Corpus Christi, Texas. Only veterans could join, so no one could easily call it disloyal or Communist, and it fought discrimination boldly. When Félix Longoria, a soldier killed in the Philippines, was refused burial in Three Rivers, Texas, the Forum, helped by Senator Lyndon Johnson, won him burial at Arlington National Cemetery.

In 1960, leaders including Bert Corona founded MAPA to back Mexicano candidates, and Viva Kennedy clubs helped elect John F. Kennedy.

Word help: register = sign up to vote · disloyal = not faithful to one's country · candidate = a person running for office

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 7, “The Mexican American Generation,” pp. 231–235.

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 6: How did the rise of a Mexican American intelligentsia challenge older narratives and help prepare the way for later activism?

IN MY WORDS: How did Mexican American scholars change the way history was told?

Reading KScholars Who Changed the Story

Between 1940 and 1965, Mexican American scholars, an intelligentsia, began writing their people into history. Ernesto Galarza earned a PhD at Columbia, organized farmworkers, and wrote studies showing that big growers gained the most from the Bracero Program. Carlos E. Castañeda, a University of Texas historian, wrote 18 books, including Our Catholic Heritage in Texas, and was the first major historian of Mexicanos in the Southwest. George I. Sánchez wrote Forgotten People (1940), pioneered bilingual education, and served as LULAC's president in 1941. They mixed research with activism and set an example for later scholar-activists.

Historians still argue about the middle class of this era. Chicano historians of the 1960s and 1970s often called its members vendidos (sellouts) who cared only about themselves. Later historians argue that groups like LULAC, CSO, and the G.I. Forum won real gains, ending most legal forms of discrimination by the mid-1960s.

Word help: intelligentsia = educated thinkers and writers · bilingual = in two languages · activism = working for change

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 7, “The Mexican American Intelligentsia,” pp. 237–239.
