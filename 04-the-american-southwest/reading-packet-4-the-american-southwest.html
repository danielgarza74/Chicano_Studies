What did conquest change for Mexicanos?

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 1: How did U.S. conquest alter the legal status, political power, and racial position of Mexicanos in the Southwest after 1848?

IN MY WORDS: Why were Mexicanos treated as outsiders in a land that had been theirs?

Reading AWhy Anglos Looked Down on Mexicanos

After the U.S.-Mexican War, anti-Mexican feeling spread across the Southwest. Part of it was leftover anger from the war. In the California gold fields, Anglo miners envied successful Spanish-speaking miners and used the insult “greasers” to push them out. Land fights made Mexicanos look like foreigners at home.

Religion mattered too: many Protestants called Irish and Mexican Catholics lazy, controlled by priests, and unable to fit in. The deepest cause was race. Folklorist Arthur Campa pointed out that Mexicans were treated with prejudice above all because they were “dark-skinned people.” Racism kept most Mexicanos in the lowest-paid jobs.

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo promised protection, but the promise often failed. One researcher counted 352 lynchings in California between 1850 and 1935; 132 of the victims were Latinos.

Word help: ubiquitous = everywhere · prejudice = unfair dislike of a group · lynching = killing by a mob without a trial · menial = low-status, low-paid

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 4, “Gringos and Greasers,” pp. 95–97.

Source AA Promise in the Treaty

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo ended the U.S.-Mexican War in 1848. Article VIII describes what would happen to Mexicans living in the lands Mexico gave up.

“Mexicans now established in territories previously belonging to Mexico . . . shall be free to continue where they now reside, or to remove at any time to the Mexican Republic, retaining the property which they possess in the said territories”

Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848), Article VIII; text from the Avalon Project, Yale Law School. Gonzales, Mexicanos, discusses its protections in ch. 4, pp. 97 and 99.

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 2: How did land loss, violence, economic change, and community organization reshape Mexican life in California?

IN MY WORDS: How did Californios lose land and power, and how did they respond?

Reading BGold, Vigilantes, and an Unfair Tax

Gold was found in California in January 1848, and by 1849 more than 100,000 gold seekers had rushed in. Early-arriving miners from Sonora, Mexico, and South America did well, which angered many Anglo miners.

With little regular law in the camps, vigilantes acted as police and judges, and their “trials” were unfair to minorities. In 1851, in the town of Downieville, a young Mexicana known as Juanita killed an Anglo miner who was breaking into her cabin. She said it was self-defense. A crowd court made up of the dead man's friends found her guilty, and she was hanged on July 5, 1851.

In 1850, California passed the Foreign Miners' License Tax: non-citizen miners had to pay $20 a month. Hispanic and Asian miners were charged, but Europeans were not. By 1851, more than two-thirds of the 15,000 Mexicano miners in the southern mines had been driven out, even some Californios the treaty was supposed to protect.

Word help: vigilante = a person who enforces “law” without legal power · license = official permission · non-citizen = not a U.S. citizen

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 4, “California,” pp. 97–99.

Reading CHow Californios Lost Their Ranchos

Disappointed miners began settling on the huge ranchos that Spain and Mexico had granted to Californios. Congress answered with the Land Act of 1851. Every landowner now had to prove ownership before a federal commission. The burden of proof was on the owner, not the government. Old Mexican deeds had vague borders, and most rancheros spoke no English, so they had to hire Anglo lawyers.

About three-fourths of the claims were finally approved, but cases dragged on; appeals took about 17 years on average. Rancheros were “land rich but money poor,” so many paid their lawyers with land. Others later lost land through fraud.

In northern California, Californios lost most of their land by the late 1850s. In the south, high beef prices helped for a while. Then droughts in 1862–1864 and heavy debt ruined many ranchers. In the 1880s a railroad land boom brought thousands of newcomers. Mexicanos became a small minority, and most now worked low-paid jobs in construction and citrus groves.

Word help: rancho = large ranch granted by Spain or Mexico · burden of proof = the duty to prove something · appeal = asking a higher court to change a decision · fraud = cheating

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 4, “California,” pp. 99–101.

Reading DResist or Adapt?

Some Mexicanos answered injustice with crime. Historians call this “social banditry”: outlaws whose crimes grew out of being conquered and mistreated. The most famous bandit, Joaquín Murietta, was probably a legend built from several real men named Joaquín, but the story became a symbol of resistance. Tiburcio Vásquez was real. The Mexicano community protected him until he was caught and hanged in 1875.

Still, adapting to Anglo rule was far more common than fighting it. Wealthy families often married into Anglo families. Mariano Vallejo had eight children who married Americans, and he worked closely with the newcomers. Pablo de la Guerra took a different path. He had fought against the U.S. in 1846, but after the war he accepted the new government on his own terms, built a political machine that ran Santa Barbara, and defended the rights of his people.

Most ordinary Mexicanos, about 15,000 people, had no land to lose. They found safety by living together in barrios in towns and colonias in the countryside.

Word help: accommodation = adapting to the people in power · legend = a story that may not be true · political machine = group that controls local elections · barrio = Mexican neighborhood

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 4, “California,” pp. 101–104.

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 3: How did mining, migration, labor demand, and racial hierarchy shape the Mexicano experience in territorial Arizona?

IN MY WORDS: Why did Anglo-Mexicano cooperation in Tucson break down after 1880?

Reading EArizona: Partners, Then Strangers

Southern Arizona became part of the United States through the Gadsden Purchase. In 1853, Mexico sold about 30,000 square miles for roughly $10 million. Tucson was then a small town of about 500 people, and Apache raids were a constant danger, so some residents welcomed U.S. soldiers.

Before 1880, Anglos and Mexicanos in Tucson got along better than anywhere else in the Southwest. Anglos were few (168 of 925 people in 1860), so they had to fit into the Mexicano community. Many married Mexicanas, and both groups saw the Apache as a common enemy. Mexicano freighters hauled goods by wagon between Sonora, New Mexico, and Tucson. Estevan Ochoa ran the most successful freighting company and was elected mayor of Tucson in 1875.

The Southern Pacific Railroad reached Tucson on March 20, 1880. Trade now ran east-west, and wagon freighting died. A copper-mining boom brought big companies and many Easterners who were less tolerant. Mexicano businessmen lost their wealth, and the friendly relations of earlier years faded.

Word help: freighting = hauling goods by wagon · territory = U.S. land not yet a state · tolerant = accepting of differences · corporate = run by big companies

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 4, “Arizona,” pp. 104–107.

Reading FLeaders Organize in Tucson

As hostility grew, educated Mexicano leaders stepped forward. Mariano Samaniego held many local offices and encouraged Mexicanos to vote. Carlos Velasco was a lawyer and politician from Sonora who fled to Tucson for good in 1877. In 1878 he started a Spanish-language newspaper, El Fronterizo, which promoted Mexican culture and ideas.

In the 1890s, an economic depression increased anti-Mexican feeling. In 1894 the American Protective Association, a national anti-immigrant group, appeared in Tucson. Velasco, supported by Samaniego, answered by founding the Alianza Hispano-Americana on January 14, 1894, with about forty community leaders. It included both workers and businessmen. Its goal was to protect the rights of Mexicanos. By the late 1930s it had over 17,000 members.

The Alianza was a mutualista, or mutual-aid society. Mutualistas gave members emergency loans, sickness and burial insurance, help finding jobs, and community celebrations such as Cinco de Mayo.

Word help: nativist = against immigrants · mutual aid = members helping each other · depression = a time of severe economic hard times

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 4, “Arizona,” pp. 107–111.

Why did New Mexico turn out differently?

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 4: Why were Hispanos in New Mexico better able to preserve political and cultural influence, and how did Americanization challenge that influence?

IN MY WORDS: What helped Hispanos hold on to their influence, and what threatened it?

Reading GNew Mexico: Holding on

New Mexico was different. About 60,000 Mexican citizens lived there in 1848, and few Anglos moved in, so Hispanos (as Spanish-speaking New Mexicans are often called) stayed the majority until about World War II. Before 1886 the territorial legislature was mostly Hispano. Wealthy families called ricos grew richer through trade on the Santa Fe and Chihuahua trails and by buying land.

Land was the weak spot. Owners struggled to prove titles and pay taxes. After the Civil War, a group of lawyers and businessmen called the Santa Fe ring used legal tricks to grab land. Its leader, Thomas Catron, gained about two million acres. In the end, more than 80 percent of land-grant holders lost their land.

Hispanos fought back. In the late 1880s, a secret group called the Gorras Blancas (White Caps) cut fences and burned buildings to stop Anglos from taking land. The Penitentes, a lay religious brotherhood, kept village traditions alive and became so powerful that rural candidates needed their support. Hispanos lost most of their land but kept their culture's core.

Word help: Hispano = Spanish-speaking New Mexican · rico = wealthy · land grant = land given by Spain or Mexico · lay brotherhood = religious group led by ordinary members

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 4, “New Mexico,” pp. 111–118.

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 5: How did land dispossession, racial violence, economic change, and segregation transform Tejano communities in the second half of the nineteenth century?

IN MY WORDS: How did Tejanos become second-class citizens in their own state?

Reading HTexas: the Harshest Treatment

Relations were worst in Texas. In Central Texas (San Antonio), Tejano cart drivers controlled the freight trade to the Gulf Coast. In 1859, Anglo rivals attacked them in the “Cart War,” and many lost their living. Tejanos also lost land to lawsuits, taxes, and violence.

In South Texas, the cattle boom of 1865–1885 ended once railroads made long cattle drives unnecessary. Commercial farming took over. Tejano ranchers, land rich but money poor, could not keep up, and many vaqueros became low-paid farm laborers. The Texas Rangers were openly anti-Mexican and enforced the law unfairly. In 1859 Juan Cortina shot a marshal who was beating a Mexican prisoner, then led raids for his people's rights; corridos made him a hero. Corridos also honored Gregorio Cortez, who shot a sheriff during an unjust arrest in 1901.

In West Texas, Anglos took over the salt lakes Mexicanos had always used for free, leading to the Salt War of 1877 and bloody Ranger reprisals. After the railroad reached El Paso in 1881, Anglos took control; in 1884 the county seat moved, ending Tejano political power.

Word help: Tejano = Texan of Mexican descent · vaquero = cowboy · corrido = Mexican folk ballad · reprisal = punishment in revenge

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 4, “Texas,” pp. 118–124.
