How did hard times turn Mexicanos into a “problem”?

CORE OBJECTIVES

FQ 1: How did the Great Depression transform Mexicanos from needed workers into scapegoats and reshape their communities?

FQ 2: How did urbanization create new economic opportunities, discrimination, and community institutions in Southwestern barrios?

IN MY WORDS: Why did Mexicanos lose their value as workers, and what did barrio life offer them?

Reading AFrom Needed to Unwanted

The Great Depression began with the stock market crash of October 1929. By 1932 about one in four American workers had no job. In the Southwest, mines and railroads cut workers, and farmers struggled too.

For Mexicanos, the Depression changed everything. Before, big mining, railroad, and farm companies had defended Mexican immigration because they needed cheap labor. Now jobless Anglos were willing to take the hard, low-paid jobs Mexicanos used to do, and later the Dust Bowl brought thousands more white workers west. With so many people competing for work, wages dropped. Employers no longer needed Mexicano workers, so they stopped speaking up for them.

Mexicanos became scapegoats, blamed for problems they did not cause. Yet many now saw the United States as home, and many were U.S. citizens by birth.

Word help: Depression = a long period of severe economic hard times · scapegoat = a person or group unfairly blamed · expendable = easy to get rid of

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 6, “The Depression,” pp. 163–164.

Reading BLife in the Barrio

Hard times pushed many Mexicanos from farms into cities. Cities offered more kinds of odd jobs, and most New Deal relief programs were based there, although many immigrants could not qualify. In New Mexico and Colorado, about 8,000 Hispano families lost their farms, and Denver's Mexicano population tripled.

Los Angeles had 97,116 Mexicanos in 1930, the second-largest Mexican population in the world after Mexico City. Many lived in East Los Angeles barrios such as Boyle Heights. Life was hard: housing was crowded and run-down, sanitation was poor, jobs were seasonal and low-paid, and children attended segregated schools. Police often beat, framed, or even shot young Mexican men.

Still, the barrio offered security. Spanish was spoken everywhere. The Catholic Church, fiestas, dances, and picnics brought people together. Theaters showed Mexican films, and Spanish-language radio stars like Pedro J. González had huge audiences.

Word help: barrio = Mexican neighborhood · relief = government aid for the poor · segregated = separated by race · sanitation = clean water and waste removal

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 6, “Urbanization,” pp. 164–168.

FQ 3: How did the Midwestern urban experience shape Mexicano identity, acculturation, and segregation differently from the Southwest?

FQ 4: How did nativism, labor competition, and public policy construct the so-called Mexican Problem?

IN MY WORDS: How did prejudice in the Midwest and the Southwest shape Mexicano communities and U.S. policy?

Reading CMexicanos in the Midwest

Mexicanos reached the Midwest first through sugar beet fields and railroad work. During World War I, European immigration was cut off, and factories recruited workers from Texas and Mexico for steel mills, auto plants, cement works, and meatpacking. Ford began hiring Mexicanos in Detroit in 1918, paying them the same as white workers. By 1930 about 58,000 Mexicanos lived in the Midwest, mostly in cities such as Chicago, Detroit, and Gary.

Life there was different from the Southwest. Wages and schooling were often better, Mexicanos lived and worked beside European immigrants, some married outside the group, and Mexico was far away. More of them learned English.

But they still faced harsh prejudice. European immigrants feared job competition, employers used Mexicans as strikebreakers, and Chicago police were especially abusive. Pushed away, many Mexicanos turned inward, built their own churches and clubs, and proudly embraced Mexicanidad, Mexican national pride.

Word help: recruit = to sign up workers · strikebreaker = worker hired to replace strikers · acculturate = adopt some customs of a new culture · Mexicanidad = Mexican national pride

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 6, “Urbanization in the Midwest,” pp. 168–171.

Reading DInventing the “Mexican Problem”

In the 1920s, nativism, fear and dislike of immigrants, was strong. The Ku Klux Klan had millions of members, and magazines like the Saturday Evening Post ran stories about a so-called “Mexican Problem.” Writers claimed Mexicans raised crime, lowered school standards, and created slums, usually without evidence. Hollywood films spread the “greaser” stereotype. Fewer than 6 percent of Mexican immigrants had become citizens by 1930, which critics used against them.

Nativists, joined by the American Federation of Labor, pushed Congress to limit Mexican immigration. Unions feared Mexicans would lower wages and take jobs. Representative John C. Box of Texas led bills to restrict them. Southwestern businesses fought back, arguing that Mexican labor was essential, and the U.S. wanted good relations with Mexico, where Americans owned oil investments. The restriction bills failed, but in 1929 Congress made entering the country illegally a crime.

Word help: nativism = favoring native-born people over immigrants · stereotype = unfair, oversimplified image of a group · restriction = a limit

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 6, “The “Mexican Problem”,” pp. 171–173.

Reading ERepatriation: Pushed Out

With jobs gone and hostility growing, Mexico looked safer. During the 1930s about one-third of the Mexicano population left the country. Perhaps half did not leave by choice.

In 1931 the federal government launched a deportation drive in Southern California, and scare tactics alone drove 50,000 to 75,000 people out by the end of the year. Welfare offices often acted like immigration offices, pressuring families on relief to leave. Los Angeles lost about a third of its Mexican-origin residents.

Many of those sent away were American-born children, U.S. citizens, who had no real choice when their parents were deported. Their rights were ignored.

Word help: repatriation = sending people back to their home country · deportation = forced removal

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 6, “Repatriation,” pp. 173–174.

Reading FThe Dust Bowl Arrives

In the mid-1930s, drought and dust storms wrecked farms on the southern Great Plains. Starting in 1935, caravans of poor white families, often called “Okies,” headed west. About 350,000 reached California, and many settled in farm valleys.

The newcomers moved into the same farm jobs Mexicanos had done. In 1929 Anglos were only 20 percent of California's migrant farmworkers; by 1936 they were about 85 percent. Many Okies brought Southern racial attitudes, and tension between the groups lasted for decades. When Okies moved up to better jobs in packing sheds, canneries, and cotton gins, they tried to keep those jobs for themselves.

Word help: drought = long period without rain · caravan = group traveling together

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 6, “The Dust Bowl Migration,” pp. 174–175.

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 7: Why did Mexican workers engage in labor struggles during the Depression, and why were their victories often limited?

IN MY WORDS: Why did Mexicano workers strike, and what stopped them from winning?

Reading GMexicanos on Strike

Major unions such as the American Federation of Labor organized mostly skilled white workers and largely ignored Mexicanos. So Mexicanos organized themselves, often through mutualistas. In 1915, Mexicano and Anglo miners at Clifton and Morenci, Arizona, struck for four months and won real gains, and the two groups began to work together in the miners' union.

In 1928, the Imperial Valley Workers' Union, backed by mutualistas and the Mexican consulate, led a cantaloupe strike that won most of its goals, the first strike victory in California's fields.

In 1933 there were 37 farm strikes in California involving over 47,000 workers. About 5,000 berry pickers struck in El Monte. They won a small victory because the growers were Japanese and the Los Angeles Chamber of Commerce sided with the workers, but the season was nearly over and the strikers split into rival unions. That fall, about 12,000 cotton pickers, mostly Mexicanos, struck around Corcoran. Growers called them Communists, vigilantes killed three strikers, and police sided with the growers. The strike was crushed.

Word help: union = workers' organization · consulate = office of a foreign government · red-baiting = accusing people of being Communists · vigilante = person who takes the law into their own hands

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 6, “Labor Strife,” pp. 176–180.

Reading HWhy Wins Were Hard

Mexican consuls sometimes helped strikers, and Communist-led unions like the CAWIU were the first to welcome Mexican farmworkers regardless of color. Most Mexicano workers knew little about Communism; they joined because they needed help and mainstream unions had shut them out.

The obstacles were huge. Growers formed the powerful Associated Farmers of California in 1934. Most of the public sided with business, which it saw as a defense against Communism, and racism made many people oppose farmworkers' unions. Farmworkers earned so little that they could not pay union dues. Many lacked schooling and English, which made it hard to develop leaders. Undocumented workers feared deportation, and anti-union Okies took many jobs.

Even so, Gonzales concludes that, given these barriers, it is remarkable how much Mexicano workers accomplished.

Word help: consul = official who protects a country's citizens abroad · dues = fees members pay a union · undocumented = without legal immigration papers

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 6, “Labor Strife,” pp. 180–184.

CORE OBJECTIVE

FQ 8: How did Mexicanas contribute to labor activism and community survival while challenging traditional gender roles?

IN MY WORDS: How did Mexicanas lead the fight for workers and their communities?

Reading IMexicanas Lead

In poor families, a woman's wages were often needed to survive, and during the 1930s Mexicanas became more assertive in the labor movement. In canneries, women dominated some unions.

Luisa Moreno, born in Guatemala, organized farm and cannery workers for the UCAPAWA union and became its vice president, the first woman to hold that office in a major U.S. union. In 1938 she founded El Congreso del Pueblo de Habla Española to defend the civil rights of Spanish-speaking people. She was deported in 1950. Emma Tenayuca of San Antonio began organizing in high school and in 1938 led a strike of pecan shellers, most of them Mexicanas. Josefina Fierro de Bright became the driving force behind El Congreso; about 30 percent of its activists were women.

The decade brought deportations and failed strikes, but also hope: many Mexicanos now saw the United States as home, and a growing middle class began fighting for their rights as citizens.

Word help: assertive = confident and willing to speak up · civil rights = equal rights under the law · cannery = factory that cans food

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 6, “Mexicanas and the Labor Movement,” pp. 184–186.
