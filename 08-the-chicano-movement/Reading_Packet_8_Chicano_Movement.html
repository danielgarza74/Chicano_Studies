Why a Movement Began

CORE OBJECTIVES

FQ 1: What demographic and socioeconomic conditions in the mid-1960s made Chicanismo appealing to many Mexican Americans?

FQ 2: How did the Black freedom struggle, the War on Poverty, generational change, and cultural nationalism contribute to the origins of the Chicano movement?

IN MY WORDS: Why were many Mexican Americans ready to demand change in the mid-1960s?

Reading AThe Community in the Mid-1960s

By 1960 about 3.8 million Mexicanos lived in the United States, 87 percent of them in the Southwest. About two-thirds lived in cities, often in segregated barrios. More than 85 percent were born in the U.S., and the community was young: the typical person was about 19, compared with 28 for Anglos in the Southwest.

The 1950s brought real progress. The share working as laborers fell from 42 to 30 percent. Household income rose faster than Anglo income, more families owned homes, more women worked outside the home, and the number finishing high school rose almost 75 percent.

Still, Mexicanos ranked near the bottom in schooling, jobs, and income. They averaged three to four fewer years of school than Anglos, and about half had less than eight years. They earned only 47 cents for every dollar of Anglo income, unemployment was twice as high, and over one-third lived in poverty.

Word help: barrio = Mexicano neighborhood · median age = the middle age of a group · poverty = being poor

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 8, “The Mexicano Community in the Midsixties,” pp. 246–249.

Reading BWhy a Movement Began

Life was not worse in the early 1960s than before, so why did a movement start then? The biggest spark was the Black civil rights movement. After Brown v. Board (1954) and the Montgomery bus protest (1955), Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. led nonviolent campaigns for equal rights. By 1966, more militant leaders called for “Black Power.” Their struggle encouraged other people of color, including Mexicanos, to stand up for their own rights.

President Lyndon Johnson answered the unrest with the War on Poverty: federal programs such as Job Corps, VISTA, and Head Start. Chicano pressure pushed the government to include Mexicanos, and the programs trained many new leaders.

A new generation also appeared. Young activists called themselves Chicanos and Chicanas. The older Mexican American Generation had tried to fit into the mainstream; the new generation took pride in its roots, looked to Mexico and its Indigenous past, and demanded political power.

Word help: militant = ready to fight hard for a cause · federal = run by the national government · assimilation = blending into the mainstream

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 8, “Origins of the Chicano Movement,” and “The Chicano Legacy,” pp. 245, 249–250, 275.

IN MY WORDS: How did farmworkers win a union, and what could they not win?

Reading CCésar Chávez and Dolores Huerta

César Chávez was born in 1927 near Yuma, Arizona. When his family lost its farm in the Depression, they became migrant farmworkers in California, and César left school after eighth grade. He served in the Navy in World War II, then joined a farm union. In 1952 organizer Fred Ross brought him into the Community Service Organization (CSO), where he rose to regional director. When the CSO would not focus on farmworkers, he quit in 1961 and started his own union, later called the United Farm Workers (UFW).

Dolores Huerta, born in New Mexico in 1930, was a schoolteacher in Stockton. She also trained with Fred Ross in the CSO, learning how to organize communities. She left with Chávez and became his most trusted partner, often called the union's cofounder.

The UFW was more than a union. Chávez spoke for all Mexicanos, won strong support from churches, and became a civil rights leader.

Word help: migrant = moving from place to place for work · organize = bring people together to act · union = workers who join to bargain together

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 8, “Chávez, Huerta, and the United Farm Workers,” pp. 250–252, 255.

Reading DThe Delano Strike: Victory and Limits

On September 8, 1965, Filipino grape workers led by Larry Itliong walked off the job in Delano, near Bakersfield. They wanted higher pay and recognition of their union. Most farmworkers there were Mexicanos, so Chávez's union joined and soon led the strike. Like Gandhi and Dr. King, Chávez insisted on nonviolence. The union used marches, prayer meetings, fasts, and the huelga (strike), and it won allies: the AFL-CIO, Protestant and Catholic churches, student groups, and civil rights groups.

A strike alone was weak in a small farm town, so in 1968 Chávez started a national boycott of nonunion grapes. About 17 million Americans stopped buying them, wiping out grower profits. In 1970 growers finally recognized the union and signed contracts.

The victory did not last. In 1973 growers signed with the Teamsters instead. A more conservative public mood, competition from undocumented workers, machines in the fields, and quarrels inside the union, partly over Chávez's controlling style, weakened the UFW.

Word help: boycott = refusing to buy something as a protest · recognize = officially accept · nonviolence = protest without using force

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 8, “The Delano Strike,” pp. 252–255.

Many Leaders, Many Strategies

CORE OBJECTIVES

FQ 5: How did leaders such as Reies López Tijerina, Rodolfo 'Corky' Gonzales, and José Ángel Gutiérrez offer different strategies for land, cultural, and political power?

FQ 6: How did Chicano students use walkouts, MECHA, and Chicano studies to challenge educational inequality?

IN MY WORDS: How did different leaders and students fight for power and better schools?

Reading EThree Leaders, Three Strategies

Reies López Tijerina, a former preacher, believed Hispanos in New Mexico had lost their land illegally, despite the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo. In 1963 he founded the Alianza Federal de Mercedes to win back old land grants. When courts would not listen, the Alianza occupied part of a national forest in 1966 and raided the Tierra Amarilla courthouse in 1967.

Rodolfo “Corky” Gonzales, a former boxer from Denver, focused on city youth and cultural pride. His Crusade for Justice (1966) ran a school, community center, and newspaper and led student walkouts. His 1969 youth conference adopted the Spiritual Plan of Aztlán, calling for a Chicano homeland.

José Ángel Gutiérrez of Crystal City, Texas, sought power through elections. He helped found the student group MAYO and, in 1970, La Raza Unida Party, which won city council and school board seats in Crystal City. The party did poorly statewide and had fallen apart by the mid-1970s.

Word help: land grant = land given long ago by Spain or Mexico · Aztlán = the legendary Aztec homeland, placed in the Southwest · third party = a party besides Democrats and Republicans

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 8, “Other Early Chicano Leaders,” pp. 256–262.

Reading FStudents Take Action

Chicano students faced segregated schools, racist teachers, and few chances for college. As late as 1967, no University of California campus had even 100 Mexicano students. On March 3, 1968, students walked out of several East Los Angeles high schools, led by Lincoln High teacher Sal Castro. More than 15,000 students joined these “blowouts,” and walkouts spread across the Southwest and to Chicago. Students demanded Mexicano teachers, counselors, and principals; bilingual and bicultural education; and closer ties between schools and the barrio.

In April 1969, students and professors met at UC Santa Barbara. They wrote the Plan de Santa Bárbara, which called for Chicano studies programs, and formed MECHA (El Movimiento Estudiantil de Aztlán) to unite Chicano student groups under cultural pride.

Word help: walkout = leaving school as a protest · bilingual = in two languages · bicultural = teaching two cultures

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 8, “The Chicano Student Movement,” pp. 248, 262–265.

FQ 7: How did community organizations and professionals extend the movement into politics, law, health, media, and education?

FQ 8: How did the Chicana movement confront both racism and sexism while reshaping Chicano activism and American feminism?

IN MY WORDS: How did the movement reach beyond farms and campuses, and why did Chicanas build their own movement?

Reading GThe Movement in the Community

The Brown Berets, founded in East Los Angeles in 1967, fought police brutality and drug use in the barrio and helped lead the 1968 walkouts. They also protested the Vietnam War, where Mexicanos died in high numbers. At the Chicano Moratorium march on August 29, 1970, police clashed with the crowd, and journalist Rubén Salazar was killed. In Chicago, Brown Berets opened the Benito Juárez Health Clinic in 1972.

Professionals built lasting groups. MALDEF (1967) provided lawyers and later won Plyler v. Doe (1982), which guaranteed undocumented children a public education. The Southwest Voter Registration Education Project (1975), led by Willie Velásquez, got Mexicanos to register and vote. The Association of Mexican American Educators (1965) pushed for better schooling and bilingual programs.

Word help: brutality = cruel, violent treatment · moratorium = a call to stop something (here, the war) · register = sign up to vote

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 8, “The Chicano Movement in the Community,” pp. 265–270.

Reading HThe Chicana Movement

Many men in the movement held traditional views of women. Chicanas were often kept in roles like secretary or cook and faced sexual harassment. At the 1969 Denver youth conference, mostly male delegates even declared that Chicanas did not want their own liberation. Chicanas described a triple oppression: race, class, and gender.

Women disagreed on what to do. “Loyalists” blamed Anglo society and feared that criticizing Chicano men would divide the movement. “Feminists” insisted on fighting sexism inside the community too. The two sides split openly at the first national Chicana conference in Houston in 1971.

Chicanas built their own groups, newspapers, and Chicana studies classes. Francisca Flores helped found the Comisión Femenil Mexicana Nacional (1970), and Marta Cotera helped start the Texas Women's Political Caucus (1973). When they tried working with white feminists, many were disappointed (Source B).

Word help: sexism = unfair treatment because of gender · oppression = unjust control · feminist = a person who works for women's equal rights

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 8, “The Chicana Movement,” pp. 270–273.

FQ 9: What internal divisions and external pressures caused Chicanismo to decline after the mid-1970s?

FQ 10: What were the most important achievements and limitations of the Chicano movement's legacy?

IN MY WORDS: Why did the movement fade, and what did it leave behind?

Reading IWhy the Movement Declined

By the mid-1970s Chicanismo was losing strength. Inside, groups and leaders quarreled. Personality clashes split La Raza Unida, the Brown Berets, and MAPA, and Chicanos and Chicanas divided. No single national leader emerged. Many older, wealthier Mexican Americans rejected militant tactics, and most recent immigrants stayed out because they were busy making a living.

Outside pressures mattered too. Police sometimes provoked violence and encouraged division among activists. After the Vietnam War ended in 1975, the national mood changed, and worries about the economy led Americans to care more about financial security than civil rights. All the civil rights movements of the era faded, not just the Chicano movement.

Word help: decline = grow weaker · tactics = methods used to reach a goal · provoke = deliberately cause

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 8, “The Decline of Chicanismo,” pp. 273–275.

The Legacy and the CER

Reading JThe Chicano Legacy

Historians disagree about the legacy. Critics say the movement never moved the public the way the Black freedom movement did. Gonzales takes a middle view: its impact was powerful and positive.

Its gains were real. Activist pressure got Mexicanos included in War on Poverty programs, and hundreds of community groups trace their roots to these years. Bilingual and bicultural education grew from Chicano pressure, and Chicana/o studies programs opened at more than 100 universities. Artists, starting with Luis Valdez's El Teatro Campesino, sparked a cultural renaissance. Most of all, the movement gave Mexicanos, especially young people, a lasting sense of pride.

There were limits. The farmworker union shrank, La Raza Unida faded, and few militant groups survived.

Word help: legacy = what is left behind for the future · renaissance = a rebirth of art and ideas · militant = ready to fight hard for a cause

Summary based on Gonzales, Mexicanos, ch. 8, “The Chicano Legacy,” pp. 275–277.
